February is a natural time to be honest about relationships, which makes it a good month to do the same thing with business connections. Most business owners can name their best-selling product or service without thinking. Far fewer can name their least profitable account: the client who haggles on every invoice, the buyer who pays on day 80, the relationship that quietly costs more to service than it brings in. Valentine's Day is a fair excuse for a reality check. Keep the relationships that love your bottom line back, renegotiate the ones that drain it, and end the ones that never will.

This article walks through how to fire unprofitable customers, how to measure what each account really costs, and how to renegotiate vendor contracts before your busy season gets going.

## Why revenue is the wrong way to rank your accounts

A big account feels safe. It fills your calendar, it shows up on the sales report, and it makes the business look busy. But revenue only tells you what came in. It says nothing about what went out to get it.

The hidden costs usually sit in five places:

- **Discounts and promo support** that were meant to be temporary and became permanent.
- **Delivery and fulfillment**, especially for small, frequent orders.
- **Chargebacks, returns and breakage** that show up weeks after the sale.
- **Staff time** spent on reorders, custom requests, tracking down payment and answering the same questions.
- **Slow payment**, which ties up cash you need for payroll, inventory and your next investment.

That last one matters more than owners expect. If you want to see how a slow-paying account ripples through the rest of your operation, our piece on [cash flow as the heart of long-term business success](/resources/cash-flow-is-the-heart-of-long-term-business-success) maps it out.

## Calculate cost-to-serve before you decide anything

Customer profitability analysis sounds fancier than it is. For each account, start with the revenue, subtract what it cost you to deliver the product or service, then subtract every cost you can fairly trace to that account. What's left is the contribution that account makes toward rent, salaries, insurance and your own paycheck.

Here is an illustration using made-up numbers, not client data. Assume your cost of goods is $70 per unit, and staff time is valued at $35 per hour.

| Account | Annual revenue | Cost of goods | Delivery | Promo and chargebacks | Staff time | Contribution | Margin |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Loyal repeat client (100 units at $190) | $19,000 | $7,000 | $1,200 | $0 | $2,100 (60 hrs) | $8,700 | 45.8% |
| Large volume account (400 units at $140) | $56,000 | $28,000 | $3,200 | $4,000 | $2,800 (80 hrs) | $18,000 | 32.1% |
| Discount buyer (60 units at $120) | $7,200 | $4,200 | $900 | $0 | $2,450 (70 hrs) | -$350 | -4.9% |

The large volume account looks like the star by revenue and is a solid account by contribution. The loyal repeat client is the real sweetheart, with the best margin on the list. The discount buyer is the heartbreaker: $7,200 in sales that lost money once you counted the time, and that's before the cost of waiting on payment.

This math only works if your books are clean and current. It's one reason we offer bookkeeping on a fixed monthly fee with no billing surprises: you can't analyze accounts from books that are three months behind.

## Set a threshold so the decision isn't emotional

Breaking up is hard, especially with an account that has been loyal for years or one that feels like a local friend. A written rule keeps feelings from making the call. Here is a starting point you can adjust:

1. **Margin floor.** If an account's fully loaded contribution margin stays below 15% for two consecutive quarters, it goes on the review list.
2. **Payment floor.** If an account pays more than 30 days past terms twice in a year, terms change or the account does.
3. **Time ceiling.** If an account takes more than 10% of your sales or admin hours while producing less than 5% of revenue, it needs a conversation.
4. **Strategic exceptions.** Some accounts earn a pass: a high-profile client whose name brings in new business, for example. Write down why, and revisit it each year.

Your thresholds will differ depending on your industry margins and how you sell. The point is to pick numbers while you're calm, not while a difficult invoice is sitting on your desk.

## Reprice before you leave

Not every unprofitable account deserves a goodbye. Some just deserve a new price sheet. Before ending anything, try these in order:

- **Raise the price** or reduce the discount, and give 30 to 60 days' notice.
- **Set minimums**, such as a minimum order size that justifies the delivery cost.
- **Tighten terms**, moving from net 60 to net 30 or asking for a card on file.
- **Cut the extras**, like custom work, rush orders or free extras.

Some accounts will accept the changes and become profitable. Others will walk. Both outcomes are fine. An account that leaves because you asked for a fair price was not an account worth keeping.

## End the relationship with grace and a paper trail

If repricing fails, exit kindly. You do business in a community where word travels, and the person you let go today may be on a buying committee, a new employer's team or a referral list next year.

A few habits make it go smoothly:

- **Give written notice**, typically 30 to 60 days, and check any service or supply agreement for required notice periods first.
- **Honor open orders** and outstanding commitments so you leave on good terms.
- **Keep the message short and factual.** Something like: "We're narrowing our client list to accounts where our pricing and terms work for both sides. We'll fulfill current orders through March 31, and we're grateful for the time we've worked together."
- **Offer a soft landing**, such as a referral to another provider or a one-time closeout price on remaining inventory.
- **Settle the balance** before you go, and keep copies of every notice and email.

Never blame the customer. You aren't saying they're bad. You're saying the numbers don't work, and that's the honest version anyway.

## Renegotiate vendor contracts while it's still quiet

The same discipline applies to the other side of the ledger. Vendors build price creep into contracts the way customers build discounts into habits. Late winter, before annual budgets and busy-season schedules lock in, is the cheapest time to ask for better terms.

**Core supply contracts.** Review price per unit, volume commitments and any quality-based adjustments. Ask whether pricing can flex with market conditions rather than staying fixed no matter what.

**Materials and packaging.** Inputs like these often come with volume tiers. Bring a realistic annual forecast and ask which tier you qualify for. Then ask about early-payment terms. A discount of 2% for paying within 10 days of a net-30 invoice works out to roughly a 37% annualized return, which beats almost any line of credit you could be paying down instead.

**Freight and outsourced services.** Get at least two competing quotes. Even if you stay put, a documented quote is the strongest negotiating tool you have.

**Payment processing and software.** Declined payments, processing fees and recurring platform charges add up quietly. Our post on [subscription creep](/resources/take-back-control-of-subscription-creep) shows how to audit those recurring charges in an afternoon.

### When to terminate a vendor contract

Walking away makes sense when a vendor has missed delivery windows more than once, when quality has slipped, or when you're locked into minimums you can't realistically meet. Run the math first. Suppose a supplier charges $0.12 more per unit than a competitor's quote on 24,000 units. That's $2,880 a year in overpayment. If breaking the contract costs a $1,500 fee with two years left, you recover the cost in about six months. Also check auto-renewal dates. Many contracts require 60 to 90 days' notice, and missing the window can lock you in for another year.

## Tax points to check before you cut ties

A few tax questions come up whenever a business ends a relationship, so it pays to ask before you send the letter.

**Bad debt.** If you report on the accrual method and an unpaid invoice was already counted as income, it may be deductible as a business bad debt once it becomes worthless. Cash-method taxpayers generally can't deduct unpaid sales, because the income was never recorded. Documentation matters either way, so keep records of your collection efforts. The IRS covers general business tax topics for owners on its [small business and self-employed page](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed).

**Contract termination costs.** Fees paid to exit a vendor contract may be deductible, but the treatment depends on the contract and what the payment buys. For businesses that carry inventory, some costs tied to producing goods may need to be folded into inventory instead of deducted right away.

**Multi-year effects.** Dropping a large account or changing suppliers can shift income between tax years. Erin Denney, CPA, our Tax Manager, spends much of her time on multi-year tax strategy for owner-operators, and these decisions are exactly the kind that benefit from a second set of eyes before you act, not after.

## Common Questions

**How do I know if a customer is unprofitable?**

Subtract the cost of goods, delivery, discounts, chargebacks and staff time from what the account pays you. If the remainder is below about 15% of revenue for two quarters, or negative, the account is costing you money. Late payment makes the picture worse, so track days-to-pay alongside margin.

**Should I raise prices or drop the customer?**

Raise prices or tighten terms first, with 30 to 60 days' written notice. If the account accepts, you keep a profitable customer. If it leaves, you've lost an account that wasn't paying its way. Drop it outright only when repricing won't fix the margin or the payment behavior.

**Can I deduct money a customer never paid me?**

Only in some cases. Accrual-method businesses that already recorded the sale as income can often deduct a worthless invoice as bad debt. Cash-method businesses generally can't, since the income was never counted. Keep records of invoices, reminders and any collection attempts.
