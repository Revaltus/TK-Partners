Last harvest, you probably gave a Saturday to a good cause. Maybe you poured at a school fundraiser, hauled tables for a charity auction, or sat through a long board meeting for a local nonprofit. Then tax season arrived and a fair question surfaced: does any of that count? The honest answer has two halves. The hours themselves are never deductible. But the money you spent to volunteer often is, as long as you know the rules and keep the right records.

## Why the IRS won't deduct your time

The IRS treats a charitable deduction as a gift of money or property. Your labor is neither. If you spend 40 hours organizing a fundraiser and your usual rate is $150 an hour, that is $6,000 of value you gave away, and $0 you can deduct. [IRS Publication 526](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p526) says it plainly: you cannot deduct the value of your time or services.

There is a logic to it. You never reported that $6,000 as income, so there is nothing to offset. It still stings for owner-operators, whose time is often the scarcest resource in the business. During crush, an afternoon away from the cellar has a real cost. The tax code just doesn't count it.

The other half of the misconception is the opposite mistake: assuming that because the time is out, everything else is too. It isn't.

## The out-of-pocket costs that can be deducted

Unreimbursed expenses that are directly connected to your volunteer work for a qualified organization can be deductible. "Directly connected" and "unreimbursed" are the two phrases doing the heavy lifting. Here is how common situations sort out.

| Expense | Deductible? | What to know |
|---|---|---|
| Driving to and from volunteer work | Yes | 14 cents per mile, or actual gas and oil costs |
| Parking and tolls | Yes | Keep the receipt or a note of the date and place |
| Supplies you buy for the event | Yes | Name tags, signage, serving supplies, ingredients |
| Meals and lodging on an overnight trip as a volunteer | Yes, if no significant personal pleasure or vacation element | Must be away from home overnight |
| Uniform required by the charity and not suited to everyday wear | Yes | A logo T-shirt you could wear to dinner does not qualify |
| The value of your time | No | Never deductible |
| Childcare while you volunteer | No | Not a charitable expense |
| Car insurance, depreciation, general repairs | No | Only gas and oil, or the mileage rate |
| Anything the charity paid back to you | No | You can only deduct what you absorbed |

The charitable mileage deduction is the one that surprises people, because the rate is low. It is set by statute at 14 cents per mile, not by the IRS each year like the business rate. Picture a Walla Walla tasting room owner who serves on the board of a nonprofit in Milton-Freewater. She drives a 22-mile round trip to 30 meetings and events in a year. That is 660 miles, or $92.40 of deduction. Real, but modest.

Here is a wrinkle worth knowing. If you keep records of your actual gas and oil costs, you can deduct those instead of the flat rate. At 20 miles per gallon and $4 gas, you spend about 20 cents a mile on fuel, so the actual-cost method can win. You choose one method per year for the volunteer driving, not a mix of both.

## You have to itemize for any of it to matter

Here is the catch that quietly ends most of these conversations. Volunteer expenses are charitable contributions, and charitable contributions are claimed on Schedule A. That only helps if your itemized deductions exceed the standard deduction. For 2025, the standard deduction is $15,750 for a single filer and $31,500 for married couples filing jointly.

A $92 mileage deduction will not move the needle if you are nowhere near those numbers. It can matter if you are already itemizing because of a mortgage, property taxes, large cash gifts, or medical costs. Washington has no state income tax on wages, so there is no state return to pull you toward itemizing either. The decision is purely federal.

This is also why bunching can make sense. If you give generously every year but fall just short of the threshold, concentrating two years of giving into one can push you over. Then you take the standard deduction in the off year. Your [tax planning](/what-we-do/income-tax) is the place to model that, using your real numbers instead of a guess.

## Where winery owners tend to get tripped up

Wine country has its own version of charitable life. Benefit dinners, barrel auctions, charity pours, and scholarship events fill the Walla Walla calendar, and each has a tax wrinkle.

**Benefit tickets and auction purchases.** If you pay $250 for a dinner with wine pairings that is worth $90, only the $160 above fair market value is a gift. When you pay more than $75 and receive something in return, the charity must give you a written disclosure of that value. The same logic applies to auction items. You deduct the excess over what the item is worth, not the whole bid.

**Donating your own wine.** Pouring at an event is volunteering. Handing over cases for the silent auction is a contribution of inventory. For most owners, the deduction is generally your cost, not the $38 shelf price on a bottle that costs you $14 to produce. The cost also has to come out of cost of goods sold so it is not deducted twice. Getting this wrong is a common reason an otherwise generous year turns into a messy return. Our [winery accounting team](/wineries) tracks inventory by vintage, which makes this a clean adjustment instead of a scramble.

**Sponsorships.** If you sponsor an event and get your logo on the banner, the IRS may see advertising, not a gift. Advertising can be a deductible business expense, but it belongs on a different line of the return. Which line depends on what you received in exchange.

**Mixing business and volunteering.** If you pour your wines at a charity event and also meet distributors, the trip may be partly business. Mileage for the business portion belongs in your business records. Mileage for the volunteer portion belongs on Schedule A. Do not claim the same miles twice. Getting the split right is the kind of line-drawing we do in our [tax work](/what-we-do/income-tax). It is similar in spirit to sorting out whether a vineyard dog is a [business deduction or a pet](/resources/unless-its-working-for-you-your-dog-isnt-a-tax-deduction).

## The qualified organization test

The deduction only works if the group is a qualified organization. Most 501(c)(3) charities, schools, churches, and certain government units qualify. Social clubs, civic leagues, and individuals do not, and neither does a crowdfunding page for a neighbor. Before you spend money, you can look the group up in the IRS Tax Exempt Organization Search, which is part of the [IRS Charities and Nonprofits hub](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits). It takes two minutes and prevents a disallowed deduction.

## The records that hold up

The IRS does not need you to be perfect. It needs you to be contemporaneous. A few habits cover almost everything:

- **Mileage log.** Date, destination, the charity, and miles driven. A notes app works as well as a paper notebook.
- **Receipts for supplies, parking, and travel.** Photograph them the day you buy them.
- **A statement from the charity if your unreimbursed expenses reach $250.** It should describe the services you provided, give the date, and say whether the charity gave you anything in return.
- **A note of what was reimbursed.** If the charity repays part of your costs, only the remainder counts.

None of that is hard. It just has to happen in the moment, not in February when you cannot remember if the third Thursday was the school auction or the board retreat.

## A simple way to decide what is worth tracking

Track everything during the year, since it costs you almost nothing. Then, before filing, ask three questions. Will I itemize? Do my volunteer expenses add up to anything meaningful alongside my cash gifts and any donated wine? And did anyone reimburse me? Your CPA can answer the first question in minutes with your numbers in front of them. The records you kept make the other two easy.

## Common Questions

**Can I deduct the hours I spend volunteering?**

No. The IRS does not allow a deduction for the value of your time or services, whatever your hourly rate. You can deduct unreimbursed, directly connected costs like mileage, parking, supplies, and qualifying travel, provided you itemize deductions and the organization is a qualified charity.

**How much is the charitable mileage deduction?**

The rate is 14 cents per mile for driving in service of a qualified charity. You can instead deduct actual gas and oil costs, which can be higher when fuel prices are up. Either way, keep a log with the date, destination, purpose, and miles for each trip.

**Do I need a receipt from the charity for volunteer expenses?**

If your unreimbursed expenses for one organization total $250 or more, you need a written statement from the charity describing your services, the date, and whether it gave you anything in return. You also need your own records, such as a mileage log and receipts, to support each cost.
