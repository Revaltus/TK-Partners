A winery can post a profit and still miss a payroll. It happens more often than owners expect, and it usually looks the same: a good vintage is aging in barrel, the distributor invoices are 60 days out, and a federal estimated tax payment lands the same week as harvest labor. The income statement says you are healthy. The bank balance says otherwise.

That gap is why we like to talk about cash the way a doctor talks about circulation. Profit is the photograph of your business. Cash flow is the pulse. This post walks through how to map where your cash moves, how to find the clots that slow it down, and how to schedule regular checkups before something becomes an emergency.

## Profit is the photograph, cash is the pulse

Profit measures what you earned after subtracting what it cost to earn it. Cash flow measures when the money actually arrives and when it actually leaves. For most businesses those two numbers drift apart by a few weeks. For a winery they can drift apart by years.

Here is why. When you crush fruit in October, you spend real money on grapes or vineyard costs, labor, yeast, barrels, and cellar supplies. Under cost accounting, much of that spending is not an expense yet. It becomes inventory, an asset on your balance sheet. Your profit stays intact while your bank account drops. Then the wine ages 18 to 24 months before it earns a dollar in some cases.

Both statements are telling the truth. They are just answering different questions. Profit asks, "Did we make money?" Cash flow asks, "Can we pay what is due on Friday?" Good [cash flow management for small business](/wineries) means watching both, and a winery needs to watch the second one more closely than most.

## Map how cash circulates through your winery

Before you can fix anything, you need a picture of the route cash takes. Draw it on one page. Start with where money comes in, then follow it through every stop until it leaves.

For a Walla Walla winery, the loop usually runs through these stops:

- **Tasting room sales** at the counter, strongest in spring and fall and soft in January and February.
- **Wine club shipments**, which tend to bill on release dates, so cash arrives in two or three lumps rather than evenly.
- **Distributor and wholesale invoices**, often paid 30 to 60 days after delivery.
- **Events and private tastings**, deposits first and balances later.
- **Outflows**: grape contracts, harvest labor, barrels, bottling, glass and closures, payroll, rent or mortgage, loan payments, insurance, excise and sales taxes, and owner income taxes.

The point is timing. A simple month-by-month view shows when the heart is working hardest.

| Season | Typical cash pressure | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| Late winter | Low tasting room traffic, loan and insurance payments continue | Is there enough reserve to carry January through March? |
| Spring release | Bottling and packaging costs hit before sales do | Do release-weekend sales cover bottling invoices? |
| Summer | Strong visitor traffic, vineyard labor rising | Are you banking summer surplus or spending it? |
| Harvest and crush | Heaviest outflow of the year | Are grape and labor payments scheduled against actual cash? |
| Holiday | Club shipments, gift sales, year-end tax decisions | Do estimated taxes and bonuses fit the cash you will have? |

If you run a vineyard alongside the winery, the same loop applies with its own timing for crop income and equipment. Our [winery accounting services](/wineries) are built around exactly this kind of vintage-by-vintage view, so the picture stays current instead of getting rebuilt every spring.

## Find the clots in receivables, inventory, and taxes

Circulation problems rarely come from one dramatic event. They come from a slow buildup in a few predictable places. Here are the four we look for first.

### Slow receivables

Wholesale invoices are the most common clot. A distributor that pays in 60 days instead of 30 is, in effect, borrowing from you at no interest. If you invoice $40,000 a month to wholesale accounts and the average payment takes 60 days, about $80,000 of your money is sitting in someone else's account at any given time.

Track the age of every open invoice. Anything past 45 days deserves a phone call, not another reminder email. Also check how quickly your club and e-commerce processor pays out, because holds and chargebacks can quietly delay those deposits too.

### Inventory that ties up too much cash

Inventory is the winery's biggest and most legitimate clot. Some of it is unavoidable, because aging is the product. But there is a difference between wine that is aging on purpose and wine that is sitting because production outran sales.

Look at cost per case by vintage and months of supply on hand by label. If a white you release in six months is sitting at 14 months of supply, that is cash you could have spent elsewhere. Also watch dry goods. Pallets of bottles, labels, and cartons ordered for a volume discount can cost you more in tied-up cash than the discount saves.

### Tax bills that arrive on someone else's schedule

The tax clot is the one that hurts most because it is the least flexible. Owners of pass-through wineries owe federal estimated payments on set dates throughout the year, and the [IRS small business resources](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed) outline how those quarterly payments work. Add alcohol excise taxes and sales tax collected at the tasting room, and your tax calendar is busy even before April.

The fix is not complicated. It just has to happen before the deadline. Project your taxable income each quarter, set aside the estimated amount in a separate account as sales come in, and decide on equipment purchases or retirement contributions before December, not after. ### Debt payments and small leaks

Loan principal is the sneaky one. Principal payments are real cash leaving your account, yet they do not appear as an expense on your income statement. A winery with a $48,000 annual principal payment can look fine on paper and feel squeezed in the bank.

Smaller leaks matter too. Recurring software, club-management tools, and forgotten memberships add up. We wrote about how to audit them in [taking back control of subscription creep](/resources/take-back-control-of-subscription-creep).

## See how profit and cash drift apart

Here is a simplified, illustrative example. It is not a real client, and the numbers are rounded to show the pattern.

Picture a Walla Walla winery producing about 4,000 cases a year. The income statement shows $90,000 of net profit. Looks good. Then the cash side:

| Item | Cash effect |
|---|---|
| Net profit | +$90,000 |
| Inventory increase (new vintage plus aging reds) | -$140,000 |
| Higher wholesale receivables | -$35,000 |
| Loan principal payments | -$48,000 |
| Owner estimated tax payments | -$28,000 |
| **Net change in cash** | **-$161,000** |

The business earned $90,000 and still needed $161,000 of cushion to get through the year. A line of credit, a reserve, or a slower inventory build would have covered it. Without any of those, the owner is making hard calls in August about which invoice waits.

No one did anything wrong in this picture. The winery simply grew, and growth consumes cash before it returns it. Seeing that pattern a quarter ahead is the difference between a planned borrowing decision and a scramble.

## Schedule financial checkups before a crisis hits

A checkup works because it is routine. You do not wait for chest pain to take your blood pressure. These are the three rhythms we recommend for wineries.

**Monthly: a one-hour vital signs review.** Close the books by the 15th of the following month. Review cash balance, aged receivables, inventory by vintage, and payables due in the next 30 days. This is where a fixed monthly bookkeeping fee earns its keep. Your numbers arrive on a predictable schedule, with no hourly clock running while you ask questions.

**Every month or two: a 13-week cash forecast.** This is a simple weekly grid of expected cash in and cash out for the next 13 weeks. List known events first, such as bottling invoices, payroll dates, loan payments, and club shipment dates. Then add expected tasting room and wholesale receipts. Update it every week or two as actuals come in. It is the single best tool for seeing a dip six weeks before you reach it. The [SBA's guidance on managing your business](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/manage-your-business) also covers basic cash planning habits if you want a second perspective.

**Quarterly: a tax projection.** Before each estimated payment date, sit down with your CPA and compare year-to-date results with your forecast. Adjust the payment, confirm your reserve, and decide on any purchases that would change your tax picture. Thirty minutes here can prevent a surprise at year-end.

Add an annual review in the quiet months to check vintage costing, pricing, and your borrowing plan against the next harvest.

## Common Questions

**How much cash reserve should a winery keep?**

A common target is three months of fixed costs, including payroll, loan payments, rent or mortgage, and insurance. Wineries with large harvest outlays or heavy wholesale exposure often need more. Your 13-week forecast shows the low point of the year, and your reserve should cover that number with room to spare.

**Can a profitable winery still have cash flow problems?**

Yes, and it is common. Profit counts revenue and costs when they are earned, while cash counts when money moves. Inventory aging in barrel, wholesale invoices that pay in 60 days, loan principal, and estimated taxes all drain cash without reducing profit, so a profitable winery can run short.

**What is a 13-week cash flow forecast?**

It is a weekly projection of the cash you expect to receive and pay over the next quarter. You list known bills and expected deposits by week, then update it with actuals. Its value is early warning. You see a shortfall in week seven while you still have six weeks to fix it.
