A business can post a profit and still miss a payroll. It happens more often than owners expect, and it usually looks the same: a strong quarter of sales is on the books, customer invoices are 60 days out, and a federal estimated tax payment lands the same week as a big supplier bill. The income statement says you are healthy. The bank balance says otherwise.

That gap is why we like to talk about cash the way a doctor talks about circulation. Profit is the photograph of your business. Cash flow is the pulse. This post walks through how to map where your cash moves, how to find the blockages that slow it down, and how to schedule regular checkups before something becomes an emergency.

## Profit is the photograph, cash is the pulse

Profit measures what you earned after subtracting what it cost to earn it. Cash flow measures when the money actually arrives and when it actually leaves. For most businesses those two numbers drift apart by a few weeks. For businesses that carry inventory or grow quickly, they can drift apart by months.

Here is why. When you buy materials, stock up on inventory, or invest in equipment, you spend real money. Under standard accounting, much of that spending is not an expense yet. It becomes inventory or an asset on your balance sheet. Your profit stays intact while your bank account drops. Then it can take weeks or months before that spending earns a dollar back.

Both statements are telling the truth. They are just answering different questions. Profit asks, "Did we make money?" Cash flow asks, "Can we pay what is due on Friday?" Good cash flow management means watching both, and most owners need to watch the second one more closely than they think.

## Map how cash circulates through your business

Before you can fix anything, you need a picture of the route cash takes. Draw it on one page. Start with where money comes in, then follow it through every stop until it leaves.

For most businesses, the loop usually runs through these stops:

- **Point-of-sale and online sales**, which often rise and fall with the seasons.
- **Recurring or billed revenue**, which tends to arrive on set dates, so cash comes in lumps rather than evenly.
- **Customer invoices**, often paid 30 to 60 days after delivery.
- **Deposits and progress payments**, with balances collected later.
- **Outflows**: supplier and inventory purchases, payroll, rent or mortgage, loan payments, insurance, sales and payroll taxes, and owner income taxes.

The point is timing. A simple month-by-month view shows when the heart is working hardest.

| Time of year | Typical cash pressure | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| Slow season | Sales dip while loan, rent, and insurance payments continue | Is there enough reserve to carry the slow months? |
| Ramp-up | Inventory and staffing costs hit before sales do | Will incoming sales cover the upfront bills? |
| Peak season | Strong sales, rising labor and supply costs | Are you banking the surplus or spending it? |
| Heavy spending months | Largest outflows of the year | Are payments scheduled against the cash you actually have? |
| Year-end | Bonuses, equipment decisions, and tax payments | Do estimated taxes and bonuses fit the cash you will have? |

Your own calendar will look different, and that is the point. Once you can see your pattern, the picture stays current instead of getting rebuilt every year.

## Find the blockages in receivables, inventory, and taxes

Circulation problems rarely come from one dramatic event. They come from a slow buildup in a few predictable places. Here are the four we look for first.

### Slow receivables

Unpaid customer invoices are the most common blockage. A customer that pays in 60 days instead of 30 is, in effect, borrowing from you at no interest. If you invoice $40,000 a month and the average payment takes 60 days, about $80,000 of your money is sitting in someone else's account at any given time.

Track the age of every open invoice. Anything past 45 days deserves a phone call, not another reminder email. Also check how quickly your payment processor pays out, because holds and chargebacks can quietly delay those deposits too.

### Inventory that ties up too much cash

For businesses that carry stock, inventory is often the biggest and most legitimate blockage. Some of it is unavoidable. But there is a difference between inventory you are holding on purpose and inventory that is sitting because purchasing outran sales.

Look at cost per unit and months of supply on hand for each product line. If an item is sitting at 14 months of supply, that is cash you could have spent elsewhere. Also watch bulk orders. Supplies bought for a volume discount can cost you more in tied-up cash than the discount saves.

### Tax bills that arrive on someone else's schedule

The tax blockage is the one that hurts most because it is the least flexible. Owners of pass-through businesses owe federal estimated payments on set dates throughout the year, and the [IRS small business resources](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed) outline how those quarterly payments work. Add sales tax, payroll tax, and any industry-specific taxes, and your tax calendar is busy even before April.

The fix is not complicated. It just has to happen before the deadline. Project your taxable income each quarter, set aside the estimated amount in a separate account as sales come in, and decide on equipment purchases or retirement contributions before December, not after. ### Debt payments and small leaks

Loan principal is the sneaky one. Principal payments are real cash leaving your account, yet they do not appear as an expense on your income statement. A business with a $48,000 annual principal payment can look fine on paper and feel squeezed in the bank.

Smaller leaks matter too. Recurring software, subscriptions, and forgotten memberships add up. We wrote about how to audit them in [taking back control of subscription creep](/resources/take-back-control-of-subscription-creep).

## See how profit and cash drift apart

Here is a simplified, illustrative example. It is not a real client, and the numbers are rounded to show the pattern.

Picture a growing business that stocks and sells products. The income statement shows $90,000 of net profit. Looks good. Then the cash side:

| Item | Cash effect |
|---|---|
| Net profit | +$90,000 |
| Inventory increase | -$140,000 |
| Higher customer receivables | -$35,000 |
| Loan principal payments | -$48,000 |
| Owner estimated tax payments | -$28,000 |
| **Net change in cash** | **-$161,000** |

The business earned $90,000 and still needed $161,000 of cushion to get through the year. A line of credit, a reserve, or a slower inventory build would have covered it. Without any of those, the owner is making hard calls in August about which invoice waits.

No one did anything wrong in this picture. The business simply grew, and growth consumes cash before it returns it. Seeing that pattern a quarter ahead is the difference between a planned borrowing decision and a scramble.

## Schedule financial checkups before a crisis hits

A checkup works because it is routine. You do not wait for chest pain to take your blood pressure. These are the three rhythms we recommend for business owners.

**Monthly: a one-hour vital signs review.** Close the books by the 15th of the following month. Review cash balance, aged receivables, inventory, and payables due in the next 30 days. This is where a fixed monthly bookkeeping fee earns its keep. Your numbers arrive on a predictable schedule, with no hourly clock running while you ask questions.

**Every month or two: a 13-week cash forecast.** This is a simple weekly grid of expected cash in and cash out for the next 13 weeks. List known events first, such as supplier invoices, payroll dates, and loan payments. Then add expected sales and customer payments. Update it every week or two as actuals come in. It is the single best tool for seeing a dip six weeks before you reach it. The [SBA's guidance on managing your business](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/manage-your-business) also covers basic cash planning habits if you want a second perspective.

**Quarterly: a tax projection.** Before each estimated payment date, sit down with your CPA and compare year-to-date results with your forecast. Adjust the payment, confirm your reserve, and decide on any purchases that would change your tax picture. Thirty minutes here can prevent a surprise at year-end.

Add an annual review in a quieter month to check costs, pricing, and your borrowing plan against the year ahead.

## Common Questions

**How much cash reserve should a business keep?**

A common target is three months of fixed costs, including payroll, loan payments, rent or mortgage, and insurance. Businesses with seasonal swings or large upfront purchases often need more. Your 13-week forecast shows the low point of the year, and your reserve should cover that number with room to spare.

**Can a profitable business still have cash flow problems?**

Yes, and it is common. Profit counts revenue and costs when they are earned, while cash counts when money moves. Inventory purchases, customer invoices that pay in 60 days, loan principal, and estimated taxes all drain cash without reducing profit, so a profitable business can run short.

**What is a 13-week cash flow forecast?**

It is a weekly projection of the cash you expect to receive and pay over the next quarter. You list known bills and expected deposits by week, then update it with actuals. Its value is early warning. You see a shortfall in week seven while you still have six weeks to fix it.
