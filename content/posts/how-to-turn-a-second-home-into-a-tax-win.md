After a Walla Walla harvest, the quiet stretch between December and February makes a cabin at Lake Chelan or a beach house on the Long Beach Peninsula look very appealing. A second home feels like a reward. Whether the IRS treats it as a reward or as a rental business comes down to a few day counts, and you control most of them.

That is good news. Handled with a plan, a second home can produce deductions, cover part of its own carrying costs, and still leave room for your own getaways. Here is how the second home tax rules work, with a winery owner's calendar in mind.

## The day count decides how the IRS sees your second home

The IRS sorts a vacation property into one of three buckets based on two numbers: how many days you rent it at a fair price, and how many days you use it personally. [IRS Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527) lays out the framework, and [Tax Topic 415](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc415) gives the short version.

The pivot is the so-called IRS 14 day rule for a second home. Your home counts as a personal residence if your personal use exceeds the greater of 14 days or 10% of the days you rent it at fair rental price. Cross that line and the tax treatment changes. Stay under it and the property is treated like a rental.

| Your situation | Tax treatment | What it means for you |
|---|---|---|
| Rented fewer than 15 days a year | Rental income is tax-free and not reported | No rental deductions, but mortgage interest and property taxes may still be itemized |
| Rented 15 or more days, personal use over the greater of 14 days or 10% of rental days | Mixed-use residence | Rental deductions are capped at rental income, so no tax loss |
| Rented 15 or more days, personal use at or under that threshold | Rental property | Full rental deductions and depreciation, though passive loss rules still apply |

A quick example of the 10% test: rent the home for 200 days and your threshold is 20 personal days, because 10% of 200 beats 14. Rent it for 90 days and the threshold stays at 14, because 10% of 90 is only 9.

## Which days count as personal use

More days count than most owners expect. A day is personal use if any of these people use the home:

- You or anyone who owns a share of it
- Your family members, even if they pay rent
- Anyone using it under a home swap arrangement
- Anyone paying less than a fair rental price

So the week your brother-in-law spends there at a friends-and-family rate is a personal day. So is the weekend you lend it to a distributor for free. Days you spend working on repairs and maintenance for most of the day do not count as personal use, even if family tags along. That matters in spring, when a property needs a deep clean and a deck refinish before the rental season.

## The under-15-day rental: the quietest tax perk

If you rent the home for fewer than 15 days in a year, you do not report the income. At all. Tax Topic 415 spells this out, and it is one of the few places the tax code simply lets you keep money.

This suits a property near a big event, like a lake house during a summer festival weekend, or a Walla Walla home rented out for a single release weekend when rooms in town are scarce. Rent it for 10 days at $500 a night and the $5,000 stays out of your return.

The tradeoff is that you cannot deduct rental expenses. Mortgage interest and property taxes are still itemized deductions if you itemize, subject to the usual limits, including the cap on state and local taxes and the limit on mortgage debt. Washington has no state income tax, so the cap is one more reason many owners here find the standard deduction wins anyway.

## The mixed-use home: income without a loss

Say you want to rent more often and also use the home yourself for three weeks. You land in the middle bucket. The home is treated as a residence, and rental income is fully taxable.

Expenses get split by days. Rental-day expenses are deducted in a set order: mortgage interest and property taxes first, then operating costs like utilities, cleaning, and insurance, then depreciation last. The deductions cannot push you below zero. Whatever you cannot use carries forward to a future year when the home earns more.

This is the bucket where owners get surprised. They book a few extra family weeks, expecting a deduction, and discover depreciation has been squeezed out entirely.

## The rental-property route and what it takes

Keep personal use at or under the threshold and the property is a rental. Now every allocable expense counts, including depreciation over 27.5 years on the building (land does not depreciate). Depreciation is a deduction with no cash attached, which is why a property can put money in your pocket and still show a tax loss.

A worked example, simplified for illustration. Suppose a Lake Chelan house is rented 80 nights at $325, which is $26,000 of income. You use it 12 days yourself, under the 14-day threshold. Annual costs are $14,000 in mortgage interest, $4,500 in property tax, $2,200 in insurance, and $5,000 in utilities and cleaning, plus about $14,500 of depreciation on a $400,000 building.

Rental days are 80 of 92 total days used, about 87%. That makes roughly $22,350 of the cash costs and $12,650 of the depreciation allocable to the rental. Cash flow is positive by about $3,650 after those costs. On paper, the property shows a loss near $9,000.

Now change one thing. Use the home 20 days instead of 12. You crossed the threshold, and rental days are 80 of 100. Interest and taxes allocable to rental come to about $14,800, and operating costs to about $5,760. That leaves only about $5,440 of income to absorb depreciation, and the rest carries forward. Eight extra days of personal use cost you the ability to produce a loss at all.

Your actual allocation depends on the facts of your property, so treat these figures as a model, not a promise.

## Why a winery owner's income changes the math

Here is where winery owners differ from the typical buyer. Rental losses are generally passive. Passive losses can offset passive income, not the profits from the winery you actively run. There is a special allowance of up to $25,000 for rental losses when you actively participate, but it phases out between $100,000 and $150,000 of modified adjusted gross income. Many winery owners earn above that, so the paper loss gets suspended rather than deducted.

Suspended is not lost. The loss carries forward, offsets future rental income, and is released when you sell the property. Still, if you buy expecting a big deduction this year, you may be disappointed. Buying for cash flow and long-term appreciation, with the depreciation as a bonus, is a sounder footing.

A few winery-specific planning points:

- **Crush shapes your calendar.** August through October is when you can least use the property and when it rents best in many vacation areas. Block your personal days for the quiet months and count them carefully.
- **Keep the house out of the winery entity.** Mixing a personal-use property into your tasting room's LLC can muddy both sets of books and complicate any later sale. Multi-year entity questions like this are what Erin Denney, CPA, and our tax team work through with owner-operators.
- **Rental of under 30 days has local tax rules.** Short stays in Washington generally carry sales tax and local lodging tax, and cities and counties set their own short-term rental permits. Check before your first booking.
- **Selling is a separate tax event.** A second home does not qualify for the home sale exclusion unless it was your main home for two of the last five years, and depreciation you claimed is taxed when you sell, at up to 25%.

## Second home vs investment property

The choice between a second home and an investment property is mostly a question of how you behave once you own it. A true second home leans personal: you use it often, rent it rarely, and treat the tax side as secondary. An investment property leans on rental income, with personal use capped around two weeks.

Both can be a sound purchase. The mistake is buying an investment property and then living in it half the summer, or buying a second home and budgeting as if rental income were guaranteed. Decide which one you are buying before you close, and write the plan down. ## Records that keep your position safe

The day count is only as good as your proof. Keep a simple calendar, shared with your property manager, that marks every night as one of three types: rented at fair price, personal use, or repairs and maintenance. Save booking platform reports, cleaning invoices, and the closing statement showing your purchase price and land value. If the IRS asks, the calendar does the talking.

## Common Questions

**What is the IRS 14 day rule for a second home?**

If you rent a home for fewer than 15 days a year, the income is tax-free and unreported. Separately, personal use above the greater of 14 days or 10% of rental days makes the home a residence, which limits rental deductions to rental income.

**Is vacation home rental income taxable?**

Yes, if you rent the property for 15 or more days. You report the income and deduct allocable expenses on Schedule E. If the home is a mixed-use residence, deductions cannot exceed income, so the rental will not generate a loss.

**Can I deduct my second home mortgage interest?**

Often, yes. Interest on a second home is generally deductible as an itemized deduction, within the mortgage debt limit. If you rent the home out enough, the rental portion is deducted against rental income instead, following the allocation rules.
