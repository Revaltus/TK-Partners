Your yellow Lab naps by the barrel room door, greets every tasting room guest, and has never once been asked for a W-2. So can you write off the kibble? Usually, no. But a few dogs in the Walla Walla Valley do earn their keep in ways the IRS recognizes, and the difference is worth understanding before you hand your bookkeeper a vet invoice.

This post covers the myth, the narrow exceptions, and how to document a legitimate dog expense so it holds up if anyone ever asks.

## Why a pet is a personal expense, not a business one

The tax code disallows personal, living, and family expenses. Food, vet visits, grooming, boarding, and the nice bed all land in that bucket when the dog is a companion. Calling the dog "the winery mascot" does not move it out.

The test for a business deduction is in [IRS Publication 535](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p535): the expense must be both ordinary and necessary for your trade or business. An ordinary expense is common in your line of work. A necessary one is helpful and appropriate for the business. A dog that greets visitors is pleasant. A dog that does a real job on the property is a different conversation.

That difference is the whole story. Nothing about owning a dog makes a cost deductible. What the dog does, and how clearly you can show it, does.

## Three narrow cases where dog costs can be deducted

**1. Service animals, as a medical expense.** [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502) allows you to include the cost of buying, training, and maintaining a guide dog or other service animal that assists a person with a visual, hearing, or other physical disability. That includes food, grooming, and veterinary care. The dog must be trained to perform tasks tied to the disability. A well-loved pet that makes you feel better does not meet that standard, and emotional support animals generally fall outside it.

There is a catch that surprises people. Medical expenses are only deductible if you itemize, and only the portion above 7.5 percent of your adjusted gross income counts. If your AGI is $120,000, the first $9,000 of medical costs gets you nothing. Married couples filing jointly face a standard deduction north of $30,000 in recent years, so itemizing takes real volume to pay off.

**2. Guard and working dogs, as a business expense.** If a dog actually protects business property or performs farm work, its costs can be ordinary and necessary. Think of a guard dog on a vineyard property with equipment sheds, a barrel storage building, or a crush pad that sits empty at night. Think of a working border collie that keeps birds and wildlife off the fruit, or a livestock guardian dog on a ranch.

**3. Dogs that are the product.** If you run a real breeding or training operation, the dogs are inventory or business assets, and the costs belong on the business return. The IRS will look at whether you operate like a business: records, a profit motive, and sales. A litter every few years from the family pet looks like a hobby.

## What about therapy dogs?

Therapy dogs are the most common point of confusion. A dog that visits hospitals or schools with you is a volunteer arrangement, and the dog's food and vet bills are personal costs. If you volunteer for a qualifying charity, you may be able to deduct mileage driven for that service at the charitable rate, currently 14 cents a mile. That is a small deduction, but it is a legitimate one, and a mileage log is all it takes.

## A winery example with real numbers

Picture a Walla Walla winery owner with a vineyard property outside town. A rural break-in last fall prompted her to bring in a guard dog that lives in a kennel and run by the equipment shed. It patrols the property overnight.

Her first-year costs:

- Food: $900
- Veterinary care: $600
- Professional training: $1,200
- Kennel and fencing: $1,500, which is a separate asset question

The running costs of $2,700 are potentially deductible as a security or vineyard operations expense. At a 24 percent federal bracket, that is roughly $650 in federal tax. The kennel and fencing may need to be depreciated, and the dog's purchase price may need to be capitalized rather than expensed. Those are the details we sort out with you before filing.

Now put the same owner's second dog, a retriever who lives in her house and rides along to the tasting room. Deduction: zero. Same species, same winery, entirely different facts.

| Dog's role | Deductible? | Where it goes | What backs it up |
|---|---|---|---|
| Guard dog at vineyard or production site | Yes, if it genuinely guards the property | Business security or operations expense | Kennel location, training records, incident history |
| Working dog deterring birds or wildlife | Yes, if used for that work | Vineyard or farm operating expense | Description of duties, vet and feed records |
| Trained service animal | Yes, if you itemize and exceed the 7.5% AGI floor | Schedule A medical expense | Physician letter, training records, vet bills |
| Tasting room greeter or family pet | No | Personal | Not applicable |
| Therapy dog on charity visits | Only your mileage, not the dog | Schedule A charitable | Mileage log, charity confirmation |

## The mixed-use problem

The hardest case is the dog that does a little of everything. It sleeps at the house, wanders the vineyard in the afternoon, and barks when the delivery truck arrives. The IRS is skeptical of this dog, and honestly, so are we.

If a dog is partly personal and partly business, only the business share is deductible, and you need a reasonable basis for the split. A dog that spends nights outside guarding a property is easier to defend than one that guards the property between naps on your sofa. If you want the deduction, set the dog up to do the job. Give it a kennel on site and a routine, and keep notes on what it does.

## How to document a dog expense so it holds up

A winery already carries plenty of paperwork, from federal and Washington State Liquor and Cannabis Board alcohol filings to inventory and cost records across vintages. A deduction with thin facts invites questions you do not need. Keep a short file:

- A one-paragraph description of the dog's job, written when you acquire the dog.
- Training certificates or trainer invoices.
- Vet and food receipts, coded to a specific account like "Security" or "Vineyard operations," never "Miscellaneous."
- Photos of the kennel, fencing, or posted signs at the property.
- Any incident log, such as break-ins, wildlife damage, or police reports.
- For a service animal, a physician's letter linking the dog's tasks to a disability.

## Entity structure matters too

If your winery operates as an LLC or S corporation, the entity should own the guard dog and pay its expenses directly. When an owner pays for a personal dog and then has the company reimburse it, the reimbursement is probably compensation or a distribution, not a business expense. Mixing the two is a quick way to muddy your books.

## Common Questions

**Can I deduct dog food and vet bills for my winery dog?**

Only if the dog does real work for the business, such as guarding a production site or deterring wildlife in the vineyard. A tasting room favorite that lives at home is a personal pet. Deductible costs include food, veterinary care, and training, but you need records showing the dog's role.

**Is an emotional support animal a tax deduction?**

Generally not. Publication 502 refers to service animals trained to assist with a visual, hearing, or other physical disability. If you believe your animal qualifies, ask a CPA before claiming it, and expect to need a physician's letter and training records. You must also itemize and clear the 7.5 percent of AGI floor.

**Can I write off my dog if I take it to work every day?**

No. Bringing a dog to work does not make it a business asset. The dog must perform a function that is ordinary and necessary for the business, like security patrols or livestock protection. Companionship and a pleasant workplace atmosphere are personal benefits, not business purposes.
