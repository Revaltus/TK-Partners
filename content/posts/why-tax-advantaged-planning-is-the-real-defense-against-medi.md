Picture the second week of crush. You slip on a wet cellar floor, and the next thing you know it's an ER visit, a surgery, and eight weeks of limited work during the most important stretch of your year. The medical bills are one problem. The lost income is another. And the bigger one is that most winery owners have their cash tied up in barrels, tanks, and inventory that won't turn back into money until it's sold.

A savings cushion helps. It just isn't enough on its own. How you plan for unexpected medical expenses, and which tax rules you use while you do it, decides whether a health scare is a bad quarter or a multi-year setback.

This post walks through the tools that do the heavy lifting: HSAs, itemized medical deductions, and flexible spending accounts. It also covers where each one fits for owner-operators in the Walla Walla Valley. This is general information, so we'll look at your own numbers together before you change anything.

## A cash cushion covers the first bill, not the whole bill

The Federal Reserve's [annual report on the economic well-being of U.S. households](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/report-economic-well-being-us-households.htm) asks adults how they would cover a sudden $400 expense, and it tracks unpaid medical bills as their own category. Medical costs show up in that research year after year, which tells you they are a common way a household's finances come undone.

For a winery owner, an emergency fund for medical bills is the right first layer. A useful target is your plan's annual out-of-pocket maximum. For 2025, a high-deductible plan caps that at $8,300 for an individual and $16,600 for a family. Keep that amount in a savings account and you can write the check for the worst-case year of covered care.

The cushion has two weaknesses. It's built from after-tax dollars, so every dollar you save costs you roughly a dollar and a third in pre-tax income. And it does nothing about the bills that arrive after the out-of-pocket maximum: out-of-network care, a dental emergency, physical therapy, or a prescription your plan won't cover. Tax-advantaged accounts attack both problems.

## HSA tax benefits: three breaks in one account

A health savings account is the most useful tool here, and it is the only account in the tax code with three advantages at once:

- Contributions are deductible.
- Growth inside the account is tax-free.
- Withdrawals are tax-free when you spend them on qualified medical expenses.

For 2025, you can contribute up to $4,300 with self-only coverage or $8,550 with family coverage. If you're 55 or older, add another $1,000. To qualify, you need a high-deductible health plan, which for 2025 means a deductible of at least $1,650 for an individual or $3,300 for a family, and you generally can't have other disqualifying coverage.

Here's what that looks like in dollars. A married winery owner in the 24% federal bracket who puts in the $8,550 family maximum cuts the federal tax bill by about $2,050. Washington has no state income tax on wages, so that federal deduction is the whole benefit for a Washington resident. Valley owners who live across the line in Oregon may see a state benefit too, and we'd check that with you.

Two features matter most to owners with lumpy income:

1. **The money never expires.** Unlike a cash cushion you might be tempted to dip into for a new press, HSA money stays with you year to year, and it's yours if you change plans.
2. **You can reimburse yourself later.** If you pay a qualified bill out of pocket this year, you can take the tax-free withdrawal in a future year, as long as the account existed when the expense happened and you kept the documentation. In a strong vintage year, many owners pay out of pocket, let the HSA grow, and reimburse themselves down the road.

How you contribute depends on how the winery is set up. Sole proprietors and partners deduct HSA contributions on their personal return. S-corporation owners with more than 2% of the stock run contributions through payroll as W-2 wages, then deduct them personally. The deduction is the same. The paperwork is different, and it's an easy one to get wrong.

## Itemized medical deductions: where the 7.5% floor works for you

The [medical expense tax deduction](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502) only applies to costs above 7.5% of your adjusted gross income, and only if you itemize. That floor is why most people never see a benefit. The standard deduction for a married couple filing jointly is now above $30,000, so itemized deductions have to clear a high bar before they matter.

Here is a realistic example. Say a winery owner and spouse have $120,000 of AGI after a year with heavy equipment depreciation. The floor is $9,000. If a surgery and follow-up care add up to $22,000 in qualified expenses, $13,000 is deductible, provided total itemized deductions beat the standard deduction. Add mortgage interest, property tax, and charitable giving, and that medical deduction can tip the whole return.

Winery income makes this planning more interesting, because AGI moves with the vintage. In a year when you bought a new bottling line and took bonus depreciation, AGI drops, the 7.5% floor drops with it, and more of your medical spending counts. In a banner year, the floor climbs. Because of that swing, timing matters:

- **Bunch elective care.** If a procedure, orthodontic work, or vision care is flexible, schedule it in the lower-income year.
- **Pay before December 31.** Medical expenses are deductible in the year you pay them, including by credit card.
- **Track the quiet items.** Insurance premiums you pay with after-tax dollars, mileage to medical appointments, and prescription costs all count under IRS Publication 502.

If you're self-employed, there is a separate break worth checking. Sole proprietors, partners, and S-corp owners with more than 2% of the stock can deduct health insurance premiums for themselves and their family in a way that doesn't depend on the 7.5% floor or on itemizing. It lowers AGI too, which helps your itemized medical deductions clear the floor.

## Flexible spending accounts: strong for your team, limited for you

A health FSA lets an employee set aside pre-tax wages for medical costs. The 2025 limit is $3,300. Funds are generally use-it-or-lose-it, though a plan can allow a carryover of up to $660 or a short grace period.

Here's the catch for owners. Sole proprietors, partners, and more-than-2% S-corp shareholders can't participate in a cafeteria plan, so an FSA usually isn't available to you personally. It is still a good benefit for your tasting room staff, cellar crew, and office team, and it costs you nothing beyond administration. It also reduces payroll tax on the wages employees set aside.

If you employ fewer than 50 full-time equivalent people, a QSEHRA (qualified small employer health reimbursement arrangement) is worth a look. It lets the winery reimburse employees tax-free for premiums and medical costs up to a set dollar amount. Seasonal hiring and part-time harvest labor affect eligibility and counts, so check your headcount before deciding.

## How the tools fit together

| Tool | Who can use it | 2025 limit | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| HSA | Owners and employees with a high-deductible plan | $4,300 self / $8,550 family (+$1,000 at 55) | Long-term backup fund and an annual deduction |
| Itemized medical deduction | Anyone who itemizes | Expenses above 7.5% of AGI | Low-income or high-cost years |
| Self-employed health insurance deduction | Sole proprietors, partners, 2% S-corp owners | Premiums paid, limited by business profit | Reducing AGI without itemizing |
| Health FSA | Employees (not most owners) | $3,300 | Predictable costs for your staff |
| QSEHRA | Employers under 50 full-time equivalent employees | Set annually by the IRS | Employer-funded reimbursement |

## A year-round medical plan for owner-operators

Medical planning works best on the same calendar as your tax planning. Here is how we'd sequence it:

1. **At open enrollment,** compare a high-deductible plan with an HSA against a traditional plan. The lower premium and the tax savings sometimes win, and sometimes a family with heavy care needs is better off paying more for a richer plan.
2. **At bookkeeping close each month,** move your HSA contribution and your cash-cushion transfer the same way you'd move a payroll tax deposit. Our fixed monthly bookkeeping fee means setting up those recurring transfers doesn't add a bill.
3. **After harvest,** model your AGI. Erin Denney, CPA, our Tax Manager, works with owner-operators on entity structuring and multi-year tax strategy, and this is the moment to decide whether to bunch medical spending into this year or the next.
4. **Before December 31,** make the final HSA decisions, pay flexible expenses, and gather documentation. Our secure digital upload keeps EOBs, receipts, and invoices in one place.

## Common Questions

**Is an emergency fund enough to avoid medical debt?**

A fund covers the first bill, but it doesn't reduce your taxes or grow tax-free. Pair it with an HSA so you're also saving $946 or more a year in federal tax on a $4,300 contribution at the 22% bracket, and so the same dollars can be withdrawn tax-free for medical care.

**Can I deduct all of my medical bills?**

No. You can deduct only qualified expenses above 7.5% of your AGI, and only if you itemize. On $120,000 of AGI, the first $9,000 of medical costs gives you no deduction. Self-employed health insurance premiums are handled separately and are not subject to that floor.

**Can a winery owner with an S corporation contribute to an HSA?**

Yes, if you have a qualifying high-deductible plan. For owners with more than 2% of the stock, contributions are reported as W-2 wages and deducted on your personal return. The payroll reporting has to be right, or the deduction can be disallowed.

**How much should I keep in cash for medical costs?**

A sound target is your plan's out-of-pocket maximum, which is $8,300 for individuals and $16,600 for families on a 2025 high-deductible plan. Keep that in cash or your HSA, then reassess each year when premiums and plan limits change.
