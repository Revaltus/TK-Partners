Most winery owners in Walla Walla have a portfolio that is balanced in exactly one direction: wine. The land, the vines, the barrels, the tasting room lease, and the brand all sit in one basket. The brokerage account on the side often gets set up once, funded when there is spare cash after crush, and then left alone for years. That quiet neglect is where returns leak out, and it is fixable with a short yearly routine.

This post covers what a balanced investment portfolio looks like when your biggest asset is a winery, why drift costs more than most owners expect, and how to rebalance without handing the IRS a bonus. We are CPAs, so fund selection belongs with your licensed investment adviser. Our side is the tax, cash flow, and long-range planning that decides whether the adviser's strategy works for you after taxes.

## Your winery is already the biggest bet in your portfolio

Start with the whole picture, not just the account statement. Say a Walla Walla owner has roughly $4.4 million in net worth. About $3.4 million of it is the vineyard acreage, production building, equipment, and aging inventory. The remaining $1 million or so sits in a brokerage and retirement accounts.

That owner is about 77% invested in one agricultural business that depends on weather, grape contracts, distribution relationships, and consumers' appetite for $40 bottles. Textbook asset allocation advice, such as "hold 60% stocks and 40% bonds," only describes the sliver outside the winery. It ignores the bigger slice.

The winery's risks also tend to arrive together. A smoky August, a hard winter freeze, or a soft economy can hit harvest yields, tasting room traffic, and wine club renewals in the same year. Tasting room visits and club shipments are discretionary spending, and discretionary spending usually softens when markets do. If your outside portfolio is stuffed with the same growth-sensitive holdings, a bad year can hit the business and the safety net at once.

Good portfolio diversification for a winery owner means the outside money should behave differently from the winery. That usually means more stability than a generic risk questionnaire would suggest.

## What drift looks like in real numbers

"Set it and forget it" fails quietly. Nothing dramatic happens. The winning part of the portfolio just keeps growing until it is a bigger share than you chose.

Here is a composite example, not a real client. An owner starts with a $700,000 outside portfolio and a target of 55% stocks, 35% bonds, and 10% cash. Five strong years later, nobody has touched it.

| Bucket | Target at start | Value after five years | Actual share |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stocks | 55% ($385,000) | $700,000 | 68.6% |
| Bonds | 35% ($245,000) | $250,000 | 24.5% |
| Cash | 10% ($70,000) | $70,000 | 6.9% |
| Total | $700,000 | $1,020,000 | 100% |

The account grew by $320,000, and the owner feels great. But the risk profile changed without a single decision. Now suppose stocks fall 30%. At the drifted mix, that is a $210,000 loss. Had the portfolio been rebalanced back to 55% stocks, the same drop would cost about $168,000. The gap is roughly $42,000, and it arrives in the same year your tasting room is probably slow.

That is the real cost of unmanaged risk. It does not show up as a fee on a statement. It shows up as a deeper hole at the worst possible moment.

## Rebalancing without handing the IRS a bonus

The usual objection to rebalancing is tax. Selling winners in a taxable account creates capital gains, so owners freeze. The answer is to rebalance in a smart order, not to skip it.

- **Use new money first.** Direct fresh contributions, dividends, and interest toward the underweight bucket. No sales, no gains.
- **Rebalance inside retirement accounts.** Trades inside an IRA or 401(k) do not trigger current tax, so shift the bulk of the adjustment there when you can.
- **Pick your lots.** In a taxable account, sell the shares with the highest cost basis, and hold anything you want to qualify for long-term treatment past one year.
- **Pair gains with losses.** If a holding is under water, selling it can offset gains elsewhere.
- **Mind the surtaxes.** Federal long-term capital gains rates run 0%, 15%, or 20% depending on income, and the 3.8% net investment income tax can stack on top above certain income thresholds. Washington also has its own capital gains excise tax on certain long-term gains above an annual threshold, with real estate sales exempt. Thresholds adjust, so confirm the current figures before you sell.

Winery income adds a wrinkle most generic guides miss: it is lumpy. A year with a short crop, a big equipment purchase, or heavy bonus depreciation can leave you in a much lower bracket than a banner year. A low-income year can be the right time to realize gains deliberately. A high-income year may be better for harvesting losses. Planning that across several years is the difference between rebalancing by accident and rebalancing on purpose.

## Match your risk tolerance to the harvest calendar

Risk tolerance investing questionnaires ask how you would feel if your portfolio dropped 20%. They do not ask whether you will need cash in October.

Winery owners carry cash needs that follow the calendar. Fruit purchases or vineyard labor come due before the wine sells. Reds may sit in barrel for 18 to 24 months before they generate revenue. Equipment loans and operating lines do not care that the market is down. If you are forced to sell investments at a bad time to cover a payroll or a bottling run, your stated risk tolerance was never the real one.

A simple structure helps:

1. **A liquidity bucket.** Six to twelve months of personal living expenses in cash or short-term holdings, kept separate from the business.
2. **A stability bucket.** Bonds or similar holdings meant to hold their value in the year the winery struggles.
3. **A growth bucket.** Stocks and other long-term holdings you can leave alone for ten years or more.

The exact percentages are a conversation for you, your adviser, and your CPA. The principle is that the first two buckets exist so the third never has to be sold in a panic. The Federal Reserve publishes [research notes on household finance and investment behavior](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/default.htm) if you want to see how households actually respond when markets drop. The [AICPA's personal financial planning resources](https://www.aicpa-cima.com/topic/personal-financial-planning) are a good primer on how CPAs think about this work.

## Where the rest of the plan connects

An asset allocation strategy only works if it fits the bigger structure around it. Three connections matter most for winery owners.

**Entity and income strategy.** How the winery is structured affects how much you take out, how it is taxed, and how much you can put into retirement plans each year. Erin Denney, CPA, our Tax Manager, works on exactly this kind of closely held business planning, including entity structuring and multi-year tax strategy for owner-operators. Getting the structure right determines how much money flows to your outside portfolio in the first place.

**Retirement contributions.** For many owners, the winery is the retirement plan, and that is a risk of its own. A deliberate habit of funding a retirement account every year, even in modest amounts, builds a second pillar that does not depend on a buyer for the business or the land.

**Estates and succession.** Land, vines, and inventory are hard to split among heirs, and Washington has its own estate tax with a much lower exemption than the federal one. A portfolio outside the winery can supply liquidity to pay taxes or buy out a sibling without forcing a sale of the vineyard. Our estates and trusts team can help you plan that early, in plain language.

## A yearly rhythm that fits the winery calendar

Rebalancing does not need to be a big production. One review a year, with a lighter check mid-year, is enough for most owners.

| When | What to do |
|---|---|
| January to March | Review last year's drift against your targets. Confirm retirement contributions and gather tax documents through our secure digital upload. |
| April to June | Compare your income projection with actual results. Decide whether this is a year to realize gains or hold. |
| After harvest, October to November | Update your income estimate with real crush and inventory numbers. Rebalance with fresh cash first. |
| December | Harvest losses if they make sense, finish contributions, and confirm your liquidity bucket is full. |

The timing is the point. After harvest you know what the year really looked like, and there is still time to act before the books close.

## Common Questions

**How often should a winery owner rebalance a portfolio?**

Once a year is enough for most people, with a quick check after any large market move. Many advisers also use a trigger, such as rebalancing when an asset class drifts five percentage points from target. Pick one rule, write it down, and stick to it so emotion does not make the call.

**Does rebalancing always trigger taxes?**

No. Directing new contributions and dividends to underweight holdings, and trading inside retirement accounts, creates no current tax. In taxable accounts, selling high-basis lots or pairing gains with losses can reduce the bill. Your CPA can model the cost before you place a trade.

**How much of my wealth should be outside the winery?**

There is no universal percentage. A useful test is whether you could cover twelve months of personal expenses plus a bad vintage's debt service without selling a vineyard block or discounting inventory. If the answer is no, the outside portfolio is probably too small or too aggressive.
