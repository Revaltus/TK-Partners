Every harvest, a Walla Walla winery owner stands in a dealership and hears the same pitch: the lease payment is lower, so the lease is smarter. Or the reverse: you'll own it, so buying is smarter. Neither line is a financial analysis. The right answer to lease vs buy a car depends on three numbers the salesperson rarely asks about: how many miles you drive, what the vehicle does to your cash in the months before you sell wine, and how much of the vehicle's cost you can deduct.

This post walks through those three, with the kinds of vehicles and routes winery owners in Walla Walla tend to have. The numbers are illustrations, not quotes, but the framework holds up.

## Start with the miles, because they set the price of a lease

A lease is a mileage contract wrapped around a car. Most advertised lease deals cap you at 10,000 to 12,000 miles a year, and overage fees commonly run 15 to 30 cents per mile. Go 6,000 miles over across a three-year term and you could owe $900 to $1,800 at turn-in.

Now picture a winery owner's actual year. A few runs to the vineyard each week. Trips to the Tri-Cities for supplies. A distributor drop in Seattle or Portland, each roughly 260 to 280 miles one way from Walla Walla. Wine club deliveries, trade events in Woodinville, a few weekends hauling cases to festivals. It is easy to land at 20,000 miles a year without feeling like you drive much.

If you log more than about 15,000 miles a year, buying usually wins before taxes even enter the picture. If you stay under 10,000, a lease deserves a serious look.

Wear matters too. Lease contracts charge for dents, torn upholstery and tire wear beyond "normal." A truck that hauls harvest bins, bottling supplies and muddy boots down gravel vineyard roads will not look normal at turn-in. Owned vehicles have no inspector waiting at the end.

## Cash flow: a lower payment can matter more in August than in March

Wineries do not earn evenly. Money goes out for fruit, labor, barrels and bottling long before wine is sold and paid for. A winery owner who needs a $60,000 truck has two very different options in that cycle:

- **Lease:** a smaller amount due at signing and a lower monthly payment, which leaves more cash for the vintage.
- **Buy:** a larger down payment and a higher payment, in exchange for equity you keep.

Cash is not free, though, and a lease payment is not "cheaper." You are paying for the portion of the vehicle's value you use, plus a finance charge, and at the end you own nothing. The question is whether the cash you keep in the business earns more than the equity you give up. For a winery adding a tank, barrels or a tasting room upgrade, the answer is sometimes yes. For one with comfortable reserves, it is often no.

Before you compare financing offers, check what auto loans currently cost. The Federal Reserve's [G.19 consumer credit report](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/g19/current/) publishes average rates, which gives you a benchmark when a dealer quotes you a number. Our piece on [why a winery's investment portfolio can't be set and forgotten](/resources/strategically-balancing-your-investment-portfolio-why-set-it) covers the other side of that tradeoff, which is what your spare cash should be doing.

## A three-year comparison with round numbers

Here is a simplified example for a $58,000 work truck. The lease assumes $3,000 due at signing and $689 a month for 36 months. The purchase assumes $6,000 down and a 60-month loan at 7%, with a payment near $1,030. The truck is assumed to be worth about $36,000 at the end of year three.

| | Lease (36 months) | Buy (hold 3 years, then sell) |
|---|---|---|
| Cash out the door | about $27,800 | about $43,100 |
| Loan balance owed at month 36 | none | about $23,000 |
| Estimated resale value | not yours | about $36,000 |
| Equity you walk away with | $0 | about $13,000 |
| Net cost over three years | about $27,800 | about $30,100 |

The lease looks about $2,300 cheaper over three years, before taxes, mileage penalties or wear charges. Add 6,000 excess miles and the gap is gone. Hold the truck for six or seven years instead of three, and buying pulls far ahead, because you stop making payments while the truck keeps working.

The pre-tax numbers are close. That is why the tax treatment and your mileage usually decide it.

## How each option is deducted

The deduction depends on the business-use percentage of the vehicle. If you drive 70% for the winery and 30% personally, 70% of the eligible costs are deductible. Commuting from home to the tasting room does not count as business miles, but trips from the tasting room to a vineyard or a distributor do.

| | Lease | Buy |
|---|---|---|
| What you deduct | Business share of lease payments | Business share of depreciation, plus loan interest |
| Standard mileage option | Available, but you must use it for the entire lease | Available if chosen in the first year the vehicle is in service |
| Higher-priced vehicles | An "inclusion amount" trims the deduction | Annual depreciation caps apply to passenger vehicles |
| Sales tax in Washington | Generally spread across the payments | Due up front on the purchase price |
| At the end | Return it or buy it out | You own it, with resale value or trade-in |

The standard mileage rate was 70 cents per mile for 2025, and the IRS resets it most years. At that rate, 15,000 business miles produces a $10,500 deduction without tracking a single receipt for gas or insurance. The actual-expense method can come out higher on a new, expensive truck and lower on a cheap, efficient one. Which is better depends on the vehicle, so we run both for clients before the first tax year is locked in.

Either way, you need a mileage log with dates, destinations and purpose. A calendar entry that reads "Seattle, distributor tasting, 540 miles round trip" holds up. A round estimate at year-end does not. You can send logs and receipts through our secure digital tax process rather than emailing photos of odometers.

## Section 179 and heavy vehicles

The [Section 179 vehicle deduction](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946) lets a business expense part or all of a qualifying asset in the year it goes into service, instead of spreading the cost over several years. It is the reason a winery owner hears "buy a truck before December 31." The reality has limits:

- You need more than 50% business use. Drop below that later and some of the deduction can be recaptured.
- The deduction cannot exceed the business's taxable income for the year.
- Passenger vehicles have annual depreciation caps that are far below the sticker price of most trucks.
- Heavier SUVs over 6,000 pounds gross vehicle weight have their own, lower Section 179 cap. Certain pickups with a bed of at least six feet and some cargo vans are treated differently.

IRS Publication 946 explains the rules in detail, and the figures change annually. Do not choose a truck based on a dealer's tax-savings claim. A deduction reduces your tax bill by a percentage of the cost, so a $50,000 deduction at a 24% bracket saves about $12,000. You still spent the other $38,000.

The same "is it truly business use?" test shows up in places owners don't expect. Our post on [when a working vineyard dog counts as a deduction](/resources/unless-its-working-for-you-your-dog-isnt-a-tax-deduction) uses the same logic: the deduction follows the work, not the label.

There is also an ownership question. Should the vehicle sit in the winery entity or in your name, with you reimbursed by mileage? The answer ties into how the business is structured, which is the territory of Erin Denney, CPA, our Tax Manager, who focuses on entity structuring and multi-year tax strategy for owner-operators. A vehicle decision made in isolation can create paperwork problems later.

## A short framework you can use before you sign

1. **Estimate your annual business and personal miles.** Use last year's odometer readings and calendar. Over 15,000 miles a year leans toward buying.
2. **Decide how long you keep vehicles.** Under four years leans toward leasing. Seven years or more leans strongly toward buying.
3. **Check your cash position through harvest and bottling.** If reserves are thin until the next release, a lower upfront cost has real value.
4. **Estimate your business-use percentage honestly.** Under 50% rules out Section 179 and weakens the case for deducting much at all.
5. **Ask for the price both ways.** Get the lease and the loan quote on the same vehicle, then compare total cost, not monthly payment.
6. **Run the tax effect before signing.** A CPA can model both options against your projected income for the year.

## Common Questions

**Can I deduct a lease and a purchase the same way?**

No. A lease deduction is the business share of each payment, trimmed by an inclusion amount on higher-priced vehicles. A purchase is deducted through depreciation, possibly Section 179, plus business-share interest. You can use standard mileage with either, but a leased vehicle locks you into it for the full term.

**Is it better to buy a truck at the end of the year for the tax deduction?**

Only if the truck is needed and your business income can absorb the deduction. Section 179 cannot exceed taxable business income, and a deduction only saves the percentage of tax you pay. Buying a $60,000 truck to save $14,000 in tax still costs $46,000 more than not buying it.

**What if I use my vehicle for both personal and winery trips?**

You deduct only the business-use percentage, supported by a mileage log. Trips between the tasting room and vineyards, distributors or supply stops count. Commuting from home to your regular workplace does not. Keep the log current through the year, since reconstructing it at tax time is unreliable.
