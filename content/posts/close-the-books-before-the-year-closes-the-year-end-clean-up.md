A familiar January scene at a busy business: the year was a good one, the holiday rush is over, and the books are three months behind. The CPA opens the file and finds a $14,000 "Uncategorized" account, a savings account nobody has reconciled since August, and a family grocery run coded to "Business supplies." None of this is unusual. It is also expensive, because every hour your preparer spends untangling the year is an hour on the clock.

The fix is cheaper than the problem. A few weeks of focused clean-up in Q4 turns tax prep into a review instead of a rescue. This is the year-end bookkeeping checklist for taxes that we walk business owners through, in the order that saves the most time.

## Why messy books raise your tax prep fee

A tax return is only as clean as the ledger underneath it. When the numbers are reconciled and categorized, your preparer reviews, asks a few questions, and files. When they are not, the preparer has to rebuild the year first: chasing deposits, guessing at categories, and emailing you about charges from February that you no longer remember.

That rebuild time is the part of your tax bill you can control. The [IRS recordkeeping guidance](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/recordkeeping) is blunt that your records need to support the income and deductions on your return. If you carry inventory or make large equipment purchases, sloppy records get riskier still.

There is also a decision cost. If you only learn your real profit in February, you cannot act on it. Equipment purchases, retirement contributions, and bonus timing all need to be decided before December 31, and they need accurate numbers to be decided well.

## Reconcile every account through December

Reconciling means matching your books to the bank, line by line, and explaining every difference. Business owners tend to have more accounts than they realize, and each one is a place for money to go missing from the books.

| Account | What usually goes wrong |
|---|---|
| Operating checking | Checks written but never entered, or entered twice |
| Point-of-sale or payment processor | Deposits arrive net of card fees, so sales and fees both look wrong |
| Online sales platform | Shipping, refunds, and failed card charges never reach the ledger |
| Business credit cards | Personal charges mixed in; payments booked as expenses instead of liability reductions |
| Equipment and business loans | Whole payment expensed instead of splitting principal and interest |
| Payroll liabilities | Employee withholdings not matching the payroll provider's reports |
| Savings or reserve accounts | Interest and transfers left unrecorded |

Take the payment processor. Say you sold $9,200 over a December weekend, and the processor deposited $8,940 after fees. If the books only show the $8,940, your revenue is understated by $260 and your merchant fees are missing from the expense side. Multiply that across a year of weekends and you have a real distortion in both income and deductions.

A practical note on timing: the final reconciliation needs your December 31 statements, which arrive in early January. Do everything else in December, then reconcile the last month the day the statements land. If you upload them through our secure digital process, your CPA can pick them up the same day.

## Separate personal and business spending

Commingled spending is the most common cleanup item we see, and the easiest to prevent. Owners of closely held businesses often pay for something personal with the business card because it was in their hand. It happens. The tax problem comes when the charge sits in a deductible category.

Start with a simple rule for the clean-up: scroll through every business account and card for the year and flag anything that is not clearly business spending. Then decide how each flagged item gets recorded.

- **Personal purchase on a business account:** record it as an owner draw (or a distribution, depending on your entity), not an expense.
- **Business purchase on a personal card:** record it as an expense with an owner contribution or reimbursement, and keep the receipt.
- **Mixed trips and meals:** note who attended and the business purpose now, while you can still remember.
- **Products or services taken for personal use or gifts:** record them at cost as an owner draw or the appropriate gift or promotion entry, rather than burying them in marketing or supplies.

Owner draws are not a penalty. They are simply the honest label. A deduction that does not survive review costs far more than the draw ever would, and a clean line between personal and business spending also protects the liability separation your entity is supposed to provide.

If you have not opened a dedicated business card and account, December is a good month to do it. Going forward, the separation takes care of itself.

## Fix the miscategorized transactions that pile up

Generic bookkeeping software guesses at categories, and real business spending rarely fits the guesses. These are the entries we most often move.

**Inventory and production materials.** Products you resell, or materials that go into what you sell, usually belong in inventory or cost of goods sold, not general supplies. Miscategorizing them makes your gross margin look wrong and can make tax-year cost of goods inaccurate.

**Direct labor and job costs.** Depending on your business, labor and materials may belong in cost of goods sold or in operating expenses. How those costs are classified is a tax-sensitive decision for your CPA, not a default in the software.

**Equipment loan payments.** Only the interest is an expense. The principal reduces the loan balance. A vehicle or equipment payment coded entirely as an expense overstates your costs for the year.

**Shipping and fulfillment costs.** Outbound shipping, packaging, and fulfillment should be tracked separately so you can see whether those sales are actually profitable.

**Sales and other taxes.** Collected sales tax is a liability, not revenue. Any excise taxes and state filings should reconcile to what you actually paid, and a mismatch is the kind of thing that turns into a compliance question later.

**"Uncategorized" and "Ask my accountant."** Aim for zero in both by December 31. Any balance left in them is work handed to your preparer at full rate.

If you also run payroll, check the year-end calendar early. Providers set cutoffs well before December 31, and we explain why in [our post on early-December payroll deadlines](/resources/why-your-payroll-provider-sets-an-early-december-deadline-fo).

## Count the inventory and tie it to the books

If your business holds inventory, it is often the number most likely to be wrong at year-end. Schedule a physical count as close to December 31 as practical: finished goods by SKU, raw materials, and supplies. No inventory? You can skip ahead to the timeline.

Then compare the count to your inventory records. A gap of a few units is normal (breakage, samples, spoilage). A gap of a few hundred units means something was not recorded: a sale, a transfer, a write-off. Find it now, because inventory feeds cost of goods sold, and cost of goods sold feeds taxable income.

Keep the count sheets. They are the evidence behind a major balance sheet number, and they make the next year's count faster.

## A December timeline that fits a busy season

Nobody wants a big project in the weeks after the busy season. Spread it out.

| When | Task |
|---|---|
| First week of December | Download statements and run a report of uncategorized and personal-looking transactions |
| Second week | Recode miscategorized entries and label owner draws and contributions |
| Third week | Review loan balances, payroll liabilities, and sales tax accounts |
| Last week | Physical inventory count (if you carry inventory); flag open purchase decisions for your CPA |
| First week of January | Reconcile December statements and send the package through the secure portal |

This is also the window for tax moves that only work before year-end. Erin Denney, CPA, our Tax Manager, works with owner-operators on entity structure and multi-year tax strategy, and that planning only works when the books agree with the bank. Clean numbers in December make a conversation about equipment timing or retirement contributions concrete instead of speculative.

## What the clean-up saves

The savings show up in three places. Prep time shrinks, because your preparer starts from a reconciled ledger. Surprises shrink, because you know your profit before the deadline instead of after. And the January scramble disappears: no hunting for statements, no back-and-forth over a February charge.

The bigger fix is not doing this in a sprint at all. With a [fixed monthly bookkeeping fee](/what-we-do/outsourced-accounting), the reconciling and categorizing happen every month, so December is a review, and there are no billing surprises when the work gets heavy. Year-end becomes a short checklist instead of a project.

## Common Questions

**When should I start my year-end bookkeeping clean-up?**

Start in early December, or November if you have a lot of accounts. Recoding, separating personal spending, and the inventory count all need to happen before December 31, while the final bank reconciliation waits for your December statements in early January.

**What records do I need to give my CPA for a winery tax return?**

Bring reconciled bank and card statements, loan statements, payroll reports, your Dec. 31 inventory count, and sales tax and excise filings. The [IRS small business resources](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed) list what records support income and deductions.

**Can I fix last year's miscategorized transactions after the year ends?**

Yes. Books can be corrected before the return is filed, and your CPA can amend a filed return if needed. It costs more, though. Fixing entries before filing means one pass through the return instead of two, so earlier is almost always cheaper.
