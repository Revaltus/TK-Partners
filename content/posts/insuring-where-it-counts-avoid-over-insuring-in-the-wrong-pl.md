Most winery owners we talk to in Walla Walla can list their policies but not the reasoning behind them. The package came from an agent a few years back, every deductible is low, and there is a quiet worry that something big is missing. Meanwhile the loss that could actually end the business, say a cellar fire in October with the full vintage on the floor, is insured at a number nobody has checked since the last expansion.

So, how much insurance coverage do you need? Enough that no single loss you can't afford goes uninsured, and no more than that on losses you could absorb from cash. That is the whole idea. The rest of this post shows how to run the math with your own numbers.

A quick note on roles: we are CPAs, not insurance agents. We can size the exposure from your books, tax picture and net worth. A licensed agent should price and place the coverage.

## Start with what you could lose, not what you could buy

Most insurance content is a checklist of policy types: property, liability, workers' comp, umbrella, and so on. A checklist tells you what exists. It doesn't tell you how much of each you should carry, or which ones you can skip.

An insurance needs analysis flips the order. You start with your balance sheet and cash flow, list the ways you could lose money, and then ask one question of each: could we pay for this out of reserves and keep operating?

- If yes, you probably shouldn't pay a premium to transfer that risk. Carry a high deductible or none at all.
- If no, that is where insurance earns its keep, and where you should be generous with limits.

The goal is to match your coverage to your capacity to absorb a loss. Spend premium dollars on the big, unaffordable risks and stop spending them on the small ones.

## Four questions that size every policy

For each risk on your list, answer these in order:

1. **What is the realistic worst case in dollars?** Use replacement cost, not book value.
2. **How long would it take to recover?** Months of lost revenue and continuing payroll often cost more than the damaged equipment.
3. **What cash could you put toward it tomorrow?** Count liquid reserves and credit you would actually draw on, not equity in the building.
4. **Does the loss reach beyond the business?** Personal guarantees on loans and liability judgments can pull in your home and investments, which is where net worth enters the picture.

This is also where entity structure matters. An LLC or corporation limits some liability, but it does not erase a personal guarantee on an equipment loan or your own negligence. The same question comes up in every review: where does the business end and the owner's personal exposure begin?

## A Walla Walla winery example

Here is an illustrative winery. The numbers are hypothetical, but the shape will look familiar. It produces around 4,000 cases a year and carries about $600,000 of wine inventory at cost. Cellar equipment, tanks and the tasting room fit-out add roughly $250,000. Liquid reserves are $150,000, and annual cash flow after owner pay is about $220,000.

| Risk | Worst-case loss | Can reserves absorb it? | Move |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cellar fire or flood (barrels, tanks, finished wine) | $850,000 | No | Insure, and verify limit and valuation |
| Three months closed after that fire | $180,000 | No, reserves are $150,000 | Business interruption coverage |
| Guest injury or alcohol-related liability claim | $1,000,000 or more | No, and it can reach personal assets | Liability policy plus umbrella |
| Stolen laptop or POS tablet | $2,500 | Yes | Higher deductible, skip add-on coverage |
| Broken tasting room glassware | $1,500 | Yes | Self-insure |

The first three rows justify real premium dollars. The last two don't, and an agent's standard package often covers them anyway with a $500 or $1,000 deductible.

### The deductible math

Suppose moving the property deductible from $1,000 to $5,000 cuts your annual premium by $900. You are taking on $4,000 of additional exposure per claim. Divide $4,000 by $900 and you get about 4.4 years. If you expect to file a claim less often than once every four-plus years, the higher deductible wins. With $220,000 in annual cash flow, this winery can absorb a $5,000 hit without noticing.

Run this for each policy. Premium savings divided by added exposure gives you a payback period, and a payback period is easier to judge than a gut feeling.

## Where wineries tend to over-insure

Over-insuring rarely looks like too much coverage on the big risks. It looks like small, steady leaks:

- **Low deductibles everywhere.** You pay a premium every year to avoid a cost you could cover from this month's deposits.
- **Overlapping coverage.** A tasting room equipment rider on top of a property policy that already covers it, or a paid warranty on a tank pump the manufacturer already backs.
- **Policies that outlived their purpose.** A term life policy sized to a loan that has since been paid off, or coverage on a vehicle you no longer own.
- **Automatic renewals nobody reviews.** Premiums creep like any other recurring charge. Our post on [taking back control of subscription creep](/resources/take-back-control-of-subscription-creep) walks through the same audit, and insurance renewals belong on that list.

Cutting these doesn't weaken your protection. It moves money from low-value coverage to the places where a loss would hurt.

## Where wineries tend to under-insure

The gaps usually sit in the largest exposures, and they are quieter, because nothing bills you for them until a claim.

**Using book value instead of replacement cost.** Your depreciation schedule is a tax record. A stainless tank that is fully depreciated may show zero on the books and still cost tens of thousands to replace. Insure to replacement cost, and use the schedule only to know what you own.

**Inventory valuation.** Ask whether finished wine is covered at your cost or at selling price. The difference changes both the premium and the payout. If your books track cost of goods by vintage, you already have the numbers to answer this precisely.

**Business interruption limits and waiting periods.** Covering the building but not the 90 days of payroll, loan payments and rent that continue while you can't produce or pour is a common mistake. Check how long the policy pays and how long you wait before it starts.

**Exclusions you haven't read.** Spoilage, contamination, product recall, and smoke taint are often limited or excluded in a basic package. If you farm your own fruit, ask your agent separately how crop coverage treats hail, frost and freeze events.

**Liability and the umbrella.** A tasting room serves alcohol to the public. A liability judgment can exceed the primary policy limit, and the excess lands on you personally. A common rule of thumb is an umbrella limit at least equal to your net worth.

**Key person risk.** If the winemaker is also the owner, ask what happens to the business, and to the loan covenants, if that person can't work for a year.

## Insurance and your tax return

Business insurance premiums are generally deductible as ordinary business expenses, and [IRS Publication 535](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p535) covers which types qualify. Two details matter for planning. Premiums on a life insurance policy where the business is the beneficiary generally aren't deductible. And money you set aside in a reserve to self-insure isn't deductible either, while a premium is.

That shifts the math slightly. A premium paid is a deduction, so its after-tax cost is lower than the sticker price. A reserve you hold is not, so the cost of self-insuring is real cash sitting idle. Neither argues for buying more coverage than you need, but both belong in the comparison. If you hold reserves, the same discipline applies to how they are invested, which we cover in [why a balanced investment portfolio for wineries can't be set and forgotten](/resources/strategically-balancing-your-investment-portfolio-why-set-it).

## The personal side of personal risk management planning

For most owner-operators, the winery is the largest asset on the personal balance sheet. That makes personal risk management planning inseparable from the business policies.

- **Disability income.** Your ability to work is probably your biggest earning asset. Many owners pay themselves through draws and distributions, which can complicate how a disability policy defines income. Ask before you buy.
- **Life insurance.** Size it to debts, a buy-sell agreement if you have partners, and the cash your family or estate would need. If most of your net worth is tied up in barrels and land, your heirs may need liquidity to pay estate taxes without selling the business.
- **Estate coordination.** Beneficiary designations and trust ownership affect how policy proceeds are taxed and distributed. Our [estate and trust planning](/what-we-do/estates-trusts) work often starts with exactly this question.

A good review treats the winery and the household as one balance sheet, because a loss on one side doesn't stay there.

## Common Questions

**How much insurance coverage do you need for a winery?**

Cover every loss that reserves can't absorb at full replacement cost, and let reserves absorb the rest. For most wineries that means property, business interruption and liability limits sized to your actual inventory, payroll and personal exposure, with deductibles set by payback math.

**How often should I review my coverage?**

Review once a year, and again after any trigger: a new tasting room, a larger vintage, an equipment purchase, new debt, or added distribution. Each one changes either what you could lose or what you could absorb, so the right limits move with them.

**Are insurance premiums tax deductible for a winery?**

Business insurance premiums are generally deductible as ordinary expenses. Life insurance where the business is the beneficiary, and reserves set aside for self-insurance, generally are not. IRS Publication 535 lists the rules, and we confirm how they apply to your entity before filing.
