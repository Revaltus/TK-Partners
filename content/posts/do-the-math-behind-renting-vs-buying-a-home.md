A winery owner in Walla Walla who asks "should I buy or keep renting?" is usually asking three questions at once. What will the monthly cost be? What happens to the cash I need for harvest? And will a lender count my income the way I do? Most buy-versus-rent articles answer only the first, and they answer it with a mood ("rent is throwing money away") or a market guess ("prices might drop"). Neither belongs in a financial decision.

This post treats the choice the way a CPA treats any big move: as a change to your personal financial statement. Put the numbers on the page, then decide.

## Start with your balance sheet, not the listing

Before you compare a mortgage payment to a rent check, write down what you own and owe today. Cash, brokerage and retirement accounts, your ownership stake in the winery, vehicles, and any debt, personal or business, that you have guaranteed.

The [Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/files/scf20.pdf) shows that a primary residence is the largest asset on many American households' balance sheets. For a winery owner, that has a catch. Your balance sheet is probably already lopsided. Barrels, tanks, vineyard improvements, and inventory aging in the cellar are all tied up in one business. A house adds a second large asset you cannot sell quickly.

So the first question is about liquidity. After you buy, how many months of personal expenses and business obligations could you cover without selling anything? If the honest answer is "fewer than six," the purchase may be sound but the timing needs work. If you are also weighing other big purchases, our look at [leasing versus buying a vehicle](/resources/auto-leasing-vs-buying-which-makes-more-sense) uses the same cash-flow-first approach.

## Price the true monthly cost of owning

The mortgage payment is the number everyone quotes and the least complete one. A fair comparison counts five things:

- **Interest.** The cost of borrowing. Principal is not a cost, because it builds equity, so leave it out of the comparison.
- **Property tax.** Budget roughly 1% of assessed value as a starting assumption, then check the actual rate for the parcel.
- **Insurance.** Homeowner's coverage, plus any rider if part of the property is used for the business.
- **Maintenance.** A common planning figure is 1% of the home's value per year. Older homes and rural well-and-septic properties often run higher.
- **Lost return on the down payment.** Cash you put into a house stops earning anything elsewhere. Count it.

That last item is the one people skip, and it often decides the answer.

## Run the comparison with real numbers

Here is an illustrative example. The inputs are assumptions, not a quote on any specific property, so swap in your own.

Suppose you are looking at a $550,000 home with 20% down ($110,000), a $440,000 loan at 6.75% over 30 years, and comparable rent of $2,300 a month.

| Monthly cost | Buying | Renting |
|---|---|---|
| Mortgage interest (year-one average) | $2,450 | n/a |
| Property tax (1% of value) | $458 | n/a |
| Insurance | $150 | $20 |
| Maintenance (1% of value) | $458 | n/a |
| Lost return on $110,000 at 5% | $458 | n/a |
| Rent | n/a | $2,300 |
| **Total economic cost** | **$3,974** | **$2,320** |

The actual mortgage payment is about $2,854, and roughly $4,850 of the first year's payments goes to principal. That is real savings, but it is savings you can only reach by selling or refinancing.

The gap is about $1,654 a month, or $19,850 a year. Divide that by the home's price and you get 3.6%. In year one, the home has to appreciate about 3.6% just for buying to match renting. Add roughly 6% in selling costs ($33,000) spread over a five-year hold, and the required appreciation climbs closer to 5% a year.

That does not mean buying is wrong. Rents rise, a fixed-rate loan does not, and over ten or fifteen years the math often tilts toward owning. But the timeline matters more than the market forecast. If you may sell, move, or restructure the business within five years, the same house looks very different than it does over fifteen.

## What the mortgage interest deduction saves a winery owner

Many buyers assume the deduction makes owning much cheaper. Sometimes it does. Often it barely changes anything.

[IRS Topic No. 505](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc505) explains that you can deduct home mortgage interest only if you itemize, and that the deduction applies to interest on up to $750,000 of acquisition debt for most recent loans. Washington has no state income tax, so the usual itemized building blocks are thinner than in many states: mortgage interest, a capped amount of property tax, and charitable gifts.

Using the example above, year-one interest is about $29,400 and property tax is about $5,500, for roughly $34,900 in deductions. A married couple's standard deduction is around $30,000. Itemizing only gets you about $4,900 above what you would deduct anyway. At a 24% bracket, that is roughly $1,200 of tax savings, or about $100 a month. Worth having, but it will not close a $1,654 monthly gap.

The picture can change if part of the property is genuinely used in the business, such as a tasting room, a vineyard block, or a dedicated office. Business-use portions are treated differently from personal ones, and the allocation needs to be documented before closing, not reconstructed in March. ## Your down payment competes with your barrels

In most households, the down payment competes with a vacation fund or a car. In a winery, it competes with working capital.

Think about your calendar. Crush season brings labor, fruit, and fuel costs all at once. Bottling runs need glass, closures, and labels paid up front, long before the wine sells. A new barrel program or a tank purchase can easily outweigh a down payment on its own. If you move $110,000 out of cash in August, you may be borrowing against the business line of credit by October to cover what that cash would have handled.

That creates a quiet cost. You might pay 8% or 9% on the line of credit to protect a down payment that would have earned 5% elsewhere. In that case, the true price of the house is higher than the table shows.

A few ways owners handle it:

- **Smaller down payment with a larger reserve.** Putting 10% down raises the monthly cost, but it keeps $55,000 available for the cellar.
- **Timing the closing.** Closing in winter or early spring, after the cash from fall sales has landed, keeps the purchase out of the crush squeeze.
- **Separating the buckets.** Decide in advance which dollars are personal and which are business working capital, and do not let the house pull from the second bucket.

If your investment accounts are part of the down payment, review them first. Selling holdings in a taxable account can trigger capital gains, and it can throw off your target mix. Our piece on [balancing an investment portfolio for wineries](/resources/strategically-balancing-your-investment-portfolio-why-set-it) covers how to check that before you sell anything.

## Lenders read your tax return, so your tax strategy matters

Here is the tension that surprises owner-operators. A good tax strategy lowers your taxable income, and a mortgage underwriter qualifies you on, essentially, your taxable income.

Most lenders average two years of net income from your returns. Depreciation on equipment, bonus depreciation on a new press, and aggressive expense timing all shrink that figure. A winery that grew from $400,000 to $650,000 in revenue can still show modest owner income after good tax planning. Lenders may add back certain non-cash items, such as depreciation, but they can also discount income that is volatile or that comes from a pass-through entity's retained earnings.

If a home purchase is on your horizon within two years, that is worth planning for deliberately. The decision isn't "minimize tax" or "qualify for the loan." It is a multi-year question about which years you want to look strongest on paper. Erin Denney, CPA, our Tax Manager, works on this kind of multi-year strategy and entity structure with owner-operators, and the sequencing conversation is much easier eighteen months before an application than eighteen days before.

## A short worksheet to settle the question

You can do this on one page:

1. **List your current net worth and liquid cash.** Separate personal from business.
2. **Build the five-line ownership cost** with real quotes for insurance, the property's actual tax bill, and a maintenance figure you trust.
3. **Find the rent for a comparable home** and add 3% a year for growth.
4. **Calculate the break-even appreciation** the way the table above does, for a five-year and a ten-year hold.
5. **Stress-test your reserves.** Subtract the down payment and closing costs from your cash, then run a bad harvest or a slow tasting-room summer. Do you still have room?
6. **Check the tax side.** Will itemizing help, and how will your next two returns look to a lender?

If renting wins on the numbers, that is not a failure to build wealth. It may mean the cash is better used in the winery or in diversified investments, and you can revisit the question each year. If buying wins, you walk in knowing exactly why.

## Common Questions

**Is renting better than buying for a winery owner?**

It depends on your holding period and cash needs, not on a general rule. Renting often wins when you may move within five years or need the down payment for harvest costs and equipment. Buying tends to win over a ten-year-plus horizon with stable income and a healthy cash reserve.

**Does the mortgage interest deduction make buying worth it?**

Only if your itemized deductions exceed the standard deduction. In our example, interest plus property tax beat a roughly $30,000 standard deduction by about $4,900, worth around $1,200 a year at a 24% bracket. That helps, but it rarely decides the question alone.

**How much cash should I keep after a down payment?**

A common target is six months of combined personal and essential business expenses in accessible cash. Because wineries pay for fruit, labor, and bottling before revenue arrives, many owners aim higher than that before closing.
