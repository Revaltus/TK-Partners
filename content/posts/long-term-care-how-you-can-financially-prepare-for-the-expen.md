Most Walla Walla winery owners have planned for everything a vineyard can throw at them: a spring frost, smoke in August, a short crush. Far fewer have priced out what happens if they or a spouse need daily help at 78. That gap is expensive, because long-term care costs are one of the few retirement risks that can drain a lifetime of equity in a few years.

The good news is that this risk responds well to planning. Decided a decade early, it is a manageable line in your financial picture. Decided during a hospital discharge, it is a scramble.

## Why long-term care is a funding problem, not a medical one

Long-term care means help with the basics of daily life: bathing, dressing, eating, moving safely, or supervision because of memory loss. Health insurance and Medicare largely don't pay for it. Medicare covers limited, short skilled-nursing stays after a qualifying hospital visit, not months or years of custodial care.

So the bill lands on your balance sheet. For an owner-operator, that balance sheet is usually lopsided: land, vines, buildings, barrel inventory and a tasting room, with a modest slice in cash and brokerage accounts. That shape is what makes long-term care planning costs different for winery families than for someone who simply holds a large 401(k).

## What care costs, with a winery-sized example

National cost surveys put a private nursing home room above $100,000 a year, with assisted living and memory care landing anywhere in between depending on the level of care. Local pricing varies, so get actual quotes from Walla Walla-area facilities and home-care agencies before you build a plan around any number.

Here is a hypothetical to show how the math plays out.

A couple, 62 and 60, own a tasting room and 20 acres of estate vineyard. Their net worth is about $4.5 million, but roughly $400,000 of it is liquid. One spouse needs memory care for three years at $110,000 a year. That is $330,000, or most of their accessible cash, while the other spouse still needs to live on something and the winery still needs working capital.

The pressure point is not the total net worth. It is *which* dollars pay the bill. Selling a vineyard block or pulling equity from the business to fund care can mean unfavorable timing, capital gains tax, and a disrupted succession plan.

## Three funding sources and where each fits

Most workable plans blend sources rather than relying on one.

| Source | Best use | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Long-term care insurance (traditional or hybrid life/LTC) | Transferring a six-figure risk to an insurer | Premiums rise with age; health problems can block approval |
| Veterans benefits (Aid and Attendance) | Eligible wartime veterans and surviving spouses with limited income and assets | Strict income, net worth and service rules |
| Personal savings and investments | Covering premiums, deductibles, and the early years of care | A long stay can exhaust a portfolio |

A fourth layer, Washington's own WA Cares Fund, sits underneath all three. More on that below.

## Long-term care insurance vs self-funding for land-rich owners

The question we hear most is whether to buy a policy or simply self-fund. The honest answer depends on liquidity, not on net worth alone.

**Insurance makes more sense when:**

- Your wealth is mostly in land, buildings and the business, so a care event would force asset sales.
- You want to protect a spouse's lifestyle and the next generation's ownership of the property.
- You are healthy enough to qualify, which usually means buying in your 50s or early 60s.

**Self-funding makes more sense when:**

- You hold enough liquid investments to absorb $300,000 to $500,000 without disrupting the winery.
- Your family history and health make approval uncertain or premiums unattractive.
- You'd rather keep control of the money than pay premiums for a benefit you might never use.

Hybrid policies, which pair life insurance or an annuity with a long-term care rider, appeal to owners who dislike the "use it or lose it" feel of traditional coverage. They typically cost more up front but guarantee that someone gets paid, whether through care benefits or a death benefit. Premiums and terms vary widely by insurer and age, so compare several quotes with a licensed insurance professional.

A middle path works well too: a smaller policy sized to cover a portion of care costs, with savings handling the rest. Our post on [how much insurance coverage you need](/resources/insuring-where-it-counts-avoid-over-insuring-in-the-wrong-pl) walks through sizing coverage to net worth and cash flow, and the same logic applies here.

## The veterans aid and attendance benefit

If you or your spouse served, check this before anything else. The VA's Aid and Attendance benefit is an add-on to the VA pension for wartime veterans and surviving spouses who need help with daily activities or live in a care facility. The [VA's Aid and Attendance page](https://www.va.gov/pension/aid-attendance-housebound/) lays out the current eligibility rules and how to apply.

Two cautions for business owners:

1. **The benefit is needs-based.** It looks at income and net worth, and a winery owner's holdings can push the total over the line. How business and vineyard assets count is a question to settle early, not at application time.
2. **Asset transfers can backfire.** The VA applies a look-back period to certain transfers made to qualify. Gifting vineyard acreage to children right before applying can delay or block benefits.

For veterans who qualify, the benefit can offset part of the monthly cost of care. It rarely covers all of it, which is why it works as one layer in a blend.

## Washington's WA Cares Fund

Washington residents already contribute to a state long-term care program through a payroll premium of 0.58% of wages, which winery employers withhold from employees. The program provides a capped lifetime benefit, currently $36,500 and adjusted for inflation. That is meaningful help with home care or modifications, but it covers only a fraction of one year of full-time facility care.

Treat it as a floor, not a plan. Rules and eligibility details change, so confirm current terms before you count on a specific dollar amount. Self-employed owners should also check how the program applies to them.

## The tax side of paying for care

Taxes can soften the blow if you plan for them. [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502) explains how long-term care costs fit into the medical expense deduction.

- **Qualified premiums:** Premiums for tax-qualified long-term care insurance count as medical expenses, up to age-based annual caps that rise sharply after 60. Pub 502 has the current table.
- **Care costs:** Qualified long-term care services for a chronically ill person can count as medical expenses, and so can nursing home costs when the main reason for being there is medical care.
- **The 7.5% floor:** Medical expenses are deductible only to the extent they exceed 7.5% of adjusted gross income, so a single high-cost year of care may help more than spreading expenses thin.
- **Self-employed owners:** If your winery is a sole proprietorship, partnership or S corporation, qualified premiums may be deductible through the self-employed health insurance deduction, again within the age caps. How the business is structured changes the answer, which is where multi-year tax strategy for owner-operators matters. That is work Erin Denney, CPA, our Tax Manager, does with closely held businesses.

The deduction rules are technical and the entity matters. A quick conversation before you pay a premium can mean a different deduction path than the one you assumed.

## A timeline that fits a vineyard calendar

Like pruning, the timing is most of the work.

| Age range | What to do |
|---|---|
| 50s | Price policies while you are likely to qualify. Start a dedicated care reserve inside your investment mix. |
| 60s | Decide insurance vs self-funding. Confirm veterans benefit eligibility. Review how your estate plan treats the land and the business. |
| Early 70s | Stress-test the plan against a three-year care event. Update powers of attorney and trust provisions. |
| Any age | Avoid large gifts or transfers without checking Medicaid's five-year look-back and the VA's look-back first. |

Your portfolio belongs in this conversation too. A care reserve should be invested for the time horizon you actually have, which is why we encourage a yearly rebalancing review like the one in [our post on balanced investment portfolios for wineries](/resources/strategically-balancing-your-investment-portfolio-why-set-it).

## Common Questions

**How much should a winery owner set aside for long-term care?**

A practical target is enough accessible money to cover two to three years of care without selling land or equipment, often $250,000 to $400,000 depending on the level of care. Insurance can reduce that number. We build it from your actual liquidity, not a rule of thumb.

**Is long-term care insurance worth it if most of my wealth is in land?**

Often, yes. When wealth is illiquid, a policy keeps care costs from forcing a sale of vineyard acreage or the tasting room. If you hold several hundred thousand dollars in liquid investments, self-funding may work. The deciding factor is how much cash you can free up without disrupting the winery.

**Can I qualify for VA Aid and Attendance if I own a winery?**

Possibly, but the benefit is needs-based. The VA reviews income and net worth, and business and land holdings can affect the result. Gifting assets to qualify can also trigger a look-back penalty. Review your situation with a CPA and an elder-law attorney before applying.

**Are long-term care costs tax deductible?**

Sometimes. Qualified premiums and care costs can count as medical expenses, but only the amount above 7.5% of adjusted gross income is deductible, and premiums are subject to age-based limits. Self-employed owners may have a separate premium deduction. See IRS Publication 502 and confirm with your CPA.
