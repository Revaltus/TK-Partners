That email from your payroll provider lands right as harvest wraps up and the holiday tasting weekends begin. It says something like "year-end changes must be submitted by December 5," and it is easy to file under *later*. Please don't. The deadline is not there to make your life harder. It is there so your W-2s and 1099s come out right the first time, which means no corrections, no amended returns, and no January phone calls asking why your health insurance is on the wrong line.

This post walks through why the cutoff exists and the four items winery owners in Walla Walla miss most often. It ends with a checklist you can hand to whoever runs payroll.

## Why the deadline falls in early December

Payroll providers don't just cut checks. They also prepare your W-2s, your 1099-NECs, and your fourth-quarter and annual filings. Employee W-2s are due by January 31, and so are 1099-NECs for contractors. Providers serve thousands of employers who all want changes at once, so they set an internal cutoff weeks ahead.

After that cutoff, your last few paychecks of the year are already running through a locked process. A change submitted on December 22 may not reach the final payroll. It may need a manual adjustment, or it may only be fixable after the year closes, through a corrected W-2c. The IRS charges penalties for incorrect information returns, and they grow the longer a mistake sits. Fixing a problem before the books close is almost always cheaper and calmer than fixing it afterward.

If your provider's notice names a date, treat it as your real deadline. Your CPA's review should happen a few days before it, not after.

## Bonuses and harvest pay: the pay date decides the tax year

For employees, a bonus belongs to the year it is paid, not the year it was earned. Picture a cellar team of 12 who each earn a $1,500 harvest bonus, $18,000 in total. If that money goes out December 20, it lands on this year's W-2s. If it slips to January 3, it lands on next year's, and your employees' tax picture changes with it.

Three details are worth confirming before the cutoff:

- **Withholding method.** Bonuses are supplemental wages. Many employers withhold a flat 22 percent federal rate on them, but your provider needs to know which method you want.
- **Who is included.** Tasting room staff, seasonal crush crew, and anyone who left mid-season are easy to forget.
- **Owner pay.** If you are an S-corp owner, a year-end bonus to yourself interacts with your reasonable salary and your distributions. That is a planning conversation, not a last-minute payroll tweak.

That last point is where multi-year thinking pays off. Erin Denney, CPA, our Tax Manager, spends much of her time on entity structure and multi-year tax strategy for closely held, owner-operated businesses. Owner compensation is one of the first things she looks at. The best time to raise it is early December, while there is still a payroll left to act on.

## S-corp owner health insurance belongs on your W-2

This is the item we see missed most. If your winery is an S corporation and you own more than 2 percent of it, the IRS treats you as a "2% shareholder." Health insurance premiums the company pays for you must be included in your W-2 wages (Box 1). They are generally not subject to Social Security and Medicare tax, so Boxes 3 and 5 stay unchanged.

Here is what that looks like with real numbers. Say you pay yourself a $60,000 salary and the winery covers $1,800 a month in health premiums, or $21,600 for the year. Your Box 1 should show $81,600, while Boxes 3 and 5 still show $60,000. If payroll never hears about the premiums, Box 1 reads $60,000 and your personal return has nothing to support the self-employed health insurance deduction. That deduction is the reason for the whole exercise.

The fix is simple if it happens before the cutoff. Send your provider the year-to-date premium total and the monthly amount through December. Also confirm the company, not you personally, is paying or reimbursing the premiums. If you pay out of pocket with no reimbursement through the business, the setup doesn't work the way people assume. A short call with your CPA in November clears that up.

Spouses, children, and other family members on the payroll bring their own wrinkles. Mention them.

## Company vehicle use is a taxable fringe benefit

Winery owners and managers drive a lot: vineyard visits, distributor drop-offs, a Seattle trade tasting, a trip to the bank. When the business owns or leases the vehicle and someone also uses it personally, the personal portion is a taxable fringe benefit. That includes commuting. It must be added to that person's wages, and [IRS Publication 15-B](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-publication-15-b) explains the valuation methods.

The math is not scary, but it needs a record. If your vehicle qualifies for the cents-per-mile method, the 2025 IRS rate is 70 cents per mile. Say your general manager drove 3,000 personal miles in the winery truck. That adds $2,100 to taxable wages. Without a mileage log, you are guessing, and the IRS tends to assume the personal share is higher than you would.

To get it right, pull your odometer readings and your log by the first week of December and send the personal-use total to payroll. If you are weighing a new truck or SUV for next season, our post on [leasing versus buying a vehicle](/resources/auto-leasing-vs-buying-which-makes-more-sense) covers the cash flow and deduction side. Either way, the personal-use reporting question follows you.

## Employee data, contractors, and winery-specific perks

A W-2 is only as good as the data behind it. Names that don't match Social Security records, old mailing addresses, and missing Social Security numbers are the most common reasons returns bounce back or W-2s never reach the employee. Seasonal staff are the biggest risk. Someone who worked three weeks of crush in October may have moved twice since.

Before the cutoff, confirm:

- Legal names and Social Security numbers for everyone paid this year, including seasonal workers.
- Current mailing addresses, and whether each person has agreed to electronic W-2 delivery.
- A signed W-9 for every contractor, such as a vineyard management crew, a freelance bookkeeper, or a label designer, before you issue 1099-NECs.
- Tasting room tips, including card tips paid out through payroll, so reported tips match what lands on the W-2.
- Any wine allowance or staff case program. Employee discounts on your own products are excludable only within IRS limits, and free cases beyond those limits may be taxable wages. Check [Publication 15-B](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-publication-15-b) or ask us.

Washington has no state income tax, but your quarterly state filings still depend on correct wages and hours. Errors in year-end data can ripple into those reports too.

## A year-end payroll checklist for wineries

| Item | What to send payroll | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Bonuses | Amounts, recipients, pay date, withholding method | The pay date decides the tax year |
| S-corp owner health insurance | Year-to-date and December premium totals | Puts the premiums in Box 1 so the deduction is supported |
| Company vehicle | Personal-use miles or value per driver | Adds the taxable fringe benefit to wages |
| Wine perks and staff cases | Retail value and who received them | Prevents unreported taxable wages |
| Employee records | Names, SSNs, addresses, e-delivery consent | Avoids returned W-2s and mismatches |
| Contractors | W-9s and total paid | Needed to file 1099-NECs by January 31 |
| Final payroll date | Confirmation of the last run before December 31 | Keeps pay in the intended year |

The IRS also keeps plain-language pages on [employment taxes](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/employment-taxes) and [Form W-2](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-w-2) if you want to see how the boxes fit together.

## Common Questions

**What happens if I miss my payroll provider's year-end deadline?**

Your change may not reach the final payroll, so the error shows up on W-2s or 1099s. You would then file a corrected W-2c or amended return in January or later. Corrections cost time, and penalties for incorrect information returns increase the longer they stay uncorrected.

**Do S-corp owners really have to put health insurance on the W-2?**

Yes, if you own more than 2 percent. The premiums the company pays are reported in Box 1 wages, generally without Social Security and Medicare tax. That reporting is what supports your self-employed health insurance deduction on your personal return. Leaving it off can cost you the deduction.

**Is using the winery truck to commute taxable?**

Yes. Commuting and other personal use of a company vehicle count as a taxable fringe benefit. The value, often figured per mile, is added to wages. Keep a mileage log through December and send payroll the personal-use total before the provider's cutoff.
