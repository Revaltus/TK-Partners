Ask a winery owner in Walla Walla whether a cost segregation study could help, and the answer is usually a quick no: "I don't own the building." Or the reverse: "I own it, but that's a real estate investor thing." Both answers leave money on the table. A cost segregation study for business owners is really a way of sorting what you spent on a space into the right depreciation buckets, and that sorting matters whether your name is on the deed or on a lease. The rules are more generous to some of those buckets than others, and the details change often enough that you want a CPA in the conversation before the money is spent, not after.

This guide walks through how the sorting works, what changes for owners versus tenants, where bonus depreciation and Section 179 fit, and when a study is not worth the fee. The examples lean toward wineries because that is where the interesting wrinkles show up: tasting rooms, barrel rooms, glycol lines, crush pads. The logic carries over to farms and restaurants too.

## Why renters assume a cost segregation study is not for them

Most of the cost segregation content online is written for investors who buy apartment complexes or office buildings. It talks about "the property" as if there were one owner and one tax return. So an operating business owner who rents a tasting room downtown reads it, sees nothing about their situation, and moves on.

Here is the gap. Depreciation follows *who paid for the asset*, not who holds the title. If you are a tenant and you paid for the tasting bar, the lighting, the new HVAC, the flooring, and the plumbing for a new production sink, you generally depreciate those costs on your own return. The lease may say the improvements become the landlord's property at the end of the term. That does not change who takes the deductions while you are paying for them.

The flip side matters too. If you own your building through a separate LLC, or personally, the study usually pays off on a bigger scale, but it also brings recapture, passive loss rules, and state differences that renters never face. Both sides deserve an honest look.

## How a study sorts a building into 5-, 7-, 15-, and 39-year property

By default, a commercial building is 39-year property, depreciated in a straight line. A cost segregation study, ideally prepared by engineers who walk the site and review drawings and invoices, pulls apart the pieces that the tax code treats as shorter-lived. IRS [Publication 946](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946) lays out the recovery periods and the classification rules these studies rely on.

| Recovery period | Typical items in a winery or tasting room |
|---|---|
| 5-year | Specialty electrical for equipment, tasting bar and millwork that is not part of the structure, removable fixtures, certain décor and signage |
| 7-year | Process equipment, tank-related installations, furniture, some cellar equipment |
| 15-year | Land improvements such as paving, parking lots, fencing, drainage, landscaping, plus qualified improvement property |
| 39-year | The building shell, structural walls, roof structure, standard HVAC and plumbing serving the building as a whole |

The point is not that these items cost less or more. The point is that a dollar sitting in a 5-year bucket can come off your return much sooner than a dollar sitting in the 39-year bucket. Add bonus depreciation and the shorter buckets can often be deducted in the first year.

Wineries tend to have more of the "shorter-lived" stuff than a typical office. Think of the floor drains and slab work in a production area, the dedicated power for chillers and presses, the glycol loops, the wash-down plumbing, and the barrel room's climate controls. A generic 39-year allocation lumps all of that into the building.

## A winery example for owners who hold the building

Here is an illustration with round numbers. Treat it as a teaching example, not a promise, because every building comes out differently.

Say a Walla Walla winery owner buys a combined tasting room and production building near the airport for $2.4 million. The land is worth $400,000, leaving $2.0 million of depreciable building cost. Without a study, that is roughly $51,300 a year of straight-line depreciation over 39 years, ignoring the first-year mid-month convention.

Now suppose an engineering-based study finds that 22 percent of the building cost ($440,000) is 5- or 7-year property and 8 percent ($160,000) is 15-year land improvements. The remaining $1.4 million stays at 39 years.

With 100 percent bonus depreciation available, the first-year picture changes:

- Short-life and 15-year property: $600,000 deducted up front
- The remaining 39-year portion: about $35,900
- Total first-year deduction: roughly $636,000, compared with about $51,300 without a study

That is around $585,000 of additional first-year deductions. At an assumed 32 percent combined rate, the cash tax deferred is about $187,000. A winery that just spent heavily on the purchase and the move, and whose income that year is strong, can feel that difference right away.

Notice the word *deferred*. The deductions you take early are deductions you no longer get later, and a sale can claw some of them back. More on that below.

One winery-specific wrinkle: depreciation on assets used in production may have to be folded into inventory cost under the uniform capitalization rules, rather than deducted all at once. For a winery aging wine for two or three years, part of the benefit can ride along with the inventory until the bottles sell. Your CPA should check how that applies to your production space versus the tasting room, which is retail and treated differently.

## What tenants can do with leasehold improvements and qualified improvement property

Now the other half of the audience. You rent. Maybe you lease a downtown Walla Walla storefront for a tasting room, or a warehouse bay for production. You have put money into it. What happens?

### Qualified improvement property is the starting point

Qualified improvement property, or QIP, is an improvement to the interior of an existing nonresidential building, made after the building was first placed in service. Drywall, ceilings, interior lighting, flooring, interior doors, and interior plumbing and electrical work often fit. QIP gets a 15-year recovery period, which makes it eligible for bonus depreciation.

There are exclusions. Building enlargements, elevators and escalators, and changes to the internal structural framework do not count as QIP. That is exactly where classification work earns its fee. If the tasting room expansion included a small addition, the addition falls outside QIP and defaults to a much longer life, unless a study carves out the shorter-lived items inside it.

### Honest note: the study matters less when QIP already does the heavy lifting

If your build-out is mostly QIP and bonus depreciation is at 100 percent, the incremental value of a study for a tenant can be modest. The 15-year bucket and the 5-year bucket both get fully deducted. The study adds the most when:

- The improvement includes enlargements or structural work that fell out of QIP
- Bonus depreciation is below 100 percent for your placed-in-service date
- Your state does not follow federal bonus rules
- Much of the spend is on equipment-like items buried in a contractor's lump-sum invoice

That last one is common. A contractor bills "tenant improvements, $380,000," and the invoice does not say which part was the tasting bar versus the ceiling. A cost segregation specialist can unpack that into defensible categories.

### Bonus depreciation versus Section 179

Tenants often have a choice between two tools. Bonus depreciation applies automatically to eligible property unless you elect out for a class of property. Section 179 is an election you make asset by asset, up to an annual dollar limit, and it is limited by your business income. Section 179 can apply to QIP and to certain improvements such as roofs, HVAC, fire protection, and security systems on nonresidential property, which can help owners as well as tenants.

The practical difference is control. If you want to deduct some of a build-out this year and save the rest for a year with higher income, Section 179 gives you a dial that bonus depreciation does not. If you are weighing it against a vehicle purchase in the same year, our post on [lease vs buy a car for winery owners](/resources/auto-leasing-vs-buying-which-makes-more-sense) covers how the Section 179 limits interact with other big purchases.

### Who pays for what in the lease

The lease decides who gets the deduction, so read it before the contractor starts. Three common setups look similar on paper and very different on a tax return:

1. **You pay for the improvements.** You depreciate them. If you leave early, you generally write off the remaining basis when you abandon them.
2. **The landlord pays through a tenant improvement allowance.** The landlord owns the cost and depreciates it. How the allowance is treated on your side depends on the structure, and on how the lease describes ownership of the work.
3. **A mix.** The landlord funds the shell work and you fund the specialty items. Each pays and deducts their own share, so the invoices and the lease schedule need to line up.

If you are negotiating a lease for a new tasting room, ask for the improvement ownership language and any allowance terms in writing, and have your CPA see them before signing. A rent concession traded for a bigger allowance may look better or worse after tax, depending on who gets the depreciation.

## How the bonus depreciation schedule changes the value of timing

Bonus depreciation is the reason timing matters so much. The percentage that applies depends on the date property is placed in service (and, in some years, when it was acquired). The schedule has moved more than once in recent years:

| Placed in service | Bonus depreciation percentage |
|---|---|
| 2022 | 100% |
| 2023 | 80% |
| 2024 | 60% |
| Early 2025 (property acquired before January 20, 2025) | 40% |
| Property acquired after January 19, 2025 | 100% under 2025 legislation |

Under the earlier law, bonus depreciation was scheduled to step down by 20 points a year until it disappeared. Recent legislation restored 100 percent for qualifying property acquired after January 19, 2025, and also raised Section 179 limits. Because Congress has changed these numbers repeatedly, treat any table, including this one, as a snapshot. Confirm the current percentage, effective dates, and any binding-contract rules with your CPA before you commit to a purchase or a build-out.

Why does this matter for a study? When bonus is at 100 percent, reclassifying into shorter lives has an outsized payoff, because the shorter buckets get fully deducted right away. When bonus is lower or uncertain, the study still accelerates deductions but by a smaller margin, and the fee needs to be weighed more carefully. If bonus were to shrink again, a study done in a stronger year would have captured more.

## What can go wrong: recapture, passive losses, and state differences

A study is a timing tool, so the downsides are about what comes later.

### Depreciation recapture on sale

When you sell, depreciation you claimed on 5-, 7-, and other equipment-type property (Section 1245 property) is recaptured as ordinary income, up to the gain. Depreciation on the real property portion (Section 1250) that exceeds straight-line is also handled under special rules, and unrecaptured gain on real property is taxed at up to 25 percent federally. If you plan to hold the building for decades, or pass it on, recapture may be far away or avoided entirely. If you might sell in five years, the math is different, and you should model it before ordering the study.

### Passive loss and material participation limits

A large first-year deduction can create a tax loss. Whether you can use that loss against your winery income depends on the passive activity rules. If your building sits in an LLC that rents space to your operating winery, the self-rental rules treat net rental *income* as nonpassive, but a rental *loss* generally stays passive. That means a big paper loss from a study may be suspended and carried forward rather than used this year.

There are ways through it, such as qualifying as a real estate professional (which requires meeting hour and participation tests) or restructuring how the entities are arranged. This is where entity design comes in. Erin Denney, CPA, our Tax Manager, works on entity structuring and multi-year tax strategy for owner-operators, and these questions are exactly the kind that benefit from planning across several years instead of one.

### State conformity

Washington has no personal income tax, but it does have a business and occupation tax on gross receipts, which depreciation does not reduce. So for a Washington-only owner, the federal deduction is the main event. Owners with ties to a state that has an income tax, which includes anyone living across the border in Oregon or holding property elsewhere, may find that the state does not follow federal bonus depreciation. That creates a separate state schedule and a different state result. Ask your CPA to check each state where you file.

## Is a study worth the cost? When to skip it

Engineering-based studies are priced by scope, so quotes vary with the size and complexity of the property. A rough way to decide is to compare the likely tax deferral to the fee, and then ask three questions.

**A study tends to be worth it when:**

- You bought, built, or substantially renovated a space costing roughly $500,000 or more
- The building has specialized systems, such as production plumbing, dedicated electrical, or climate control
- You expect enough taxable income to use the deductions in the next year or two
- You plan to hold the property or the improvements for years

**It tends not to be worth it when:**

- The spend is small, or mostly a standard office-style build-out that is already QIP with full bonus
- Your income is low, or losses would be suspended under the passive rules
- You expect to sell soon and recapture would erase most of the benefit
- The lease is short and the improvements are minor

A lighter option sometimes fits in the middle: a CPA-led allocation using invoices and a contractor breakdown, without a full engineering report. It is less defensible on audit than an engineered study, so the right choice depends on the dollars at stake. The [IRS Small Business and Self-Employed Tax Center](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed) is a good place to see how the agency describes depreciation records and expectations.

## Catching up on improvements you already built

Missed this the first time? You may not be stuck. If you built out a tasting room or bought a building in a past year and used the default recovery periods, a cost segregation study can still be done later. The catch-up is usually claimed through a change in accounting method (Form 3115) rather than by amending old returns, with the missed depreciation taken in a single "catch-up" adjustment in the year of the change.

This works well for owners who sat on a purchase for a few years and then had an unusually profitable vintage. It is less useful if the improvements are nearly fully depreciated already. As always, check the rules with your CPA before assuming it applies to you.

## Common Questions

**Can a tenant do a cost segregation study?**

Yes, if the tenant paid for the improvements. The tenant depreciates its own costs, so a study can reclassify a build-out into shorter-lived property. Because qualified improvement property is already 15-year and bonus eligible, the added benefit is often smaller than for building owners, so compare the fee to the expected gain first.

**Does bonus depreciation make cost segregation unnecessary?**

No. Bonus depreciation applies only to property already classified as 5-, 7-, or 15-year. A building left in the default 39-year category gets no bonus. A study identifies the shorter-lived components, which bonus can then accelerate. The two work together, and the bonus percentage in effect on your placed-in-service date sets the size of the benefit.

**What happens to cost segregation deductions when I sell the building?**

Part of the accelerated depreciation is recaptured. Gain tied to 5- and 7-year equipment-type property is generally taxed as ordinary income, and gain on the real property portion can be taxed at up to 25 percent federally. A CPA should model your expected sale before you commit, since a short hold can erase much of the benefit.
