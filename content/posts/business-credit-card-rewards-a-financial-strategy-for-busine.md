A winery runs on a strange mix of spending. There is the $40,000 barrel order in spring, the thousand small purchases at harvest, and the tasting room that somehow needs napkins every Tuesday. Put all of that on one random card and you collect a trickle of points. Match the card to the spending and you can turn the same dollars into real cash flow and a cleaner tax picture.

This post walks through how Walla Walla winery owners can build a business credit card rewards strategy on purpose, what the tax rules say about rewards, and how to keep points from turning your books into a puzzle.

## Map your spending by season before you pick a card

The best card for your winery depends on where your money goes and when. Pull twelve months of statements and sort them into categories. Then note the month each category spikes.

Here is an illustrative winery that spends about $216,000 a year on card-eligible expenses, apart from grape purchases:

| Category | Annual spend | Peak months |
|---|---|---|
| Bottles, corks, cartons, labels | $84,000 | Spring and summer bottling |
| Freight and wine club shipping | $30,000 | November and December |
| Advertising, events, and wine club marketing | $26,000 | Spring release, fall |
| Tasting room supplies and food | $22,000 | May through October |
| Software, POS, and subscriptions | $12,000 | Steady |
| Fuel, vehicle, and equipment parts | $14,000 | Harvest |
| Travel and trade shows | $10,000 | Winter and spring |
| Utilities and other | $18,000 | Steady |

The numbers are made up, but the pattern is common. Spending clusters around bottling, release season, and crush. A card that pays 2 percent on everything would earn this winery $4,320 a year. A card with a higher rate on shipping, advertising, or fuel might beat that, but only if those categories are big enough to matter. In this example they total $70,000, so a bonus category adds a few hundred dollars, not thousands.

Once you see your own table, the choice usually gets simpler. If one or two categories dominate, a category card may earn its keep. If your spending is scattered, a flat-rate card is easier to live with and easier to reconcile.

## Use the float to carry you through crush

Rewards get the attention, but the float is often worth more to a seasonal business. Depending on the card, a statement cycle plus a grace period can give you roughly 45 to 55 days between a purchase and the day cash leaves your bank account.

That matters in September and October. Harvest labor, fuel, lab work, and cleaning supplies all hit at once, while the revenue from last year's vintage trickles in through the tasting room and the wine club. Paying those costs by card and settling the full balance after the statement closes lets your cash work for you a few extra weeks.

The float only works if you pay in full. A $10,000 balance carried at 22 percent APR costs about $183 a month in interest. That wipes out a year of rewards on a $100,000 spending pace in roughly a month. Rewards are a discount for paying on time, not a reason to borrow.

## Know when a card is the wrong tool

Not every winery expense belongs on a card. Grape purchases are the big one. Growers often pay their own costs and prefer checks or ACH, and some vendors add a surcharge of 2.5 to 3 percent for card payments. If you pay $120,000 for fruit and a 3 percent surcharge applies, you spend $3,600 to earn maybe $2,400 in rewards. That is a loss with a points balance attached.

Before you move a large vendor to a card, ask three questions:

- Does the vendor charge a surcharge or raise the price for card payments?
- Does the card cap rewards in that category, or pay a lower rate on large single transactions?
- Will the charge strain your credit limit during a month when you need room for harvest costs?

Excise taxes and many regulatory payments follow their own payment rules and often come out of the bank account. Check the payment options before you plan around a card for them.

## Are credit card rewards taxable for businesses?

In most cases, no. The IRS has generally treated cash back and points earned from spending as a rebate on the purchase, not as income. You bought something, and the card issuer effectively gave you a discount.

There are exceptions worth knowing:

- **Bonuses with no purchase required.** A sign-up bonus for opening an account or making a deposit, with no spending attached, can be taxable income, and the issuer may send a Form 1099.
- **Referral payments.** Cash you receive for referring others is usually income.
- **Rewards from personal spending.** These are generally treated as a rebate, but mixing personal and business purchases creates a bigger problem than the rewards themselves.

The rebate treatment has a quiet implication. Strictly speaking, a rebate reduces the cost of what you bought. For a winery, that means rewards earned on bottling supplies are, in theory, a reduction of inventory cost. Rewards earned on advertising reduce an expense. The dollars involved are usually small, but the way you record them should be consistent and your CPA should sign off on it. The [IRS Small Business and Self-Employed Tax Center](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed) is a good starting point for the general rules, and your own situation deserves a conversation, not a guess.

Annual fees on a business card are generally deductible as a business expense. Interest on business purchases is generally deductible too, which is a poor reason to carry a balance, but it explains why the interest line shows up on your return.

## Keep rewards from muddying your books

A winery's books carry more weight than most. Inventory and cost of goods sold flow from what you spend across each vintage, and a card statement full of mixed purchases can bury that trail.

A few habits keep the picture clean:

1. **Code every card transaction to the right account.** Corks and glass belong in inventory or production costs. Tasting room napkins are an operating expense. A single "credit card" bucket hides the difference.
2. **Reconcile the card monthly, like a bank account.** The statement total should match what your books show, and any gap should be explained before the next cycle.
3. **Decide once how to record rewards.** Whether you book them as a reduction of expense or as other income when redeemed, pick a method and keep it steady. Switching every year makes your margins harder to compare.
4. **Issue employee cards with limits.** A tasting room manager and a cellar lead can each have a card with a spending cap and a category in mind. It beats reimbursing receipts weeks later.
5. **Keep personal charges off the business card.** Commingling is one of the fastest ways to turn a simple review into a long one, and it can cloud the line between owner draws and business expenses.

## Tie big purchases to your tax plan

Cards become more interesting when a large purchase is on the horizon, such as a new press, a forklift, a tasting room remodel, or a vehicle. Charging equipment can pull the deduction into the year you place it in service, and the rewards on a large purchase are real money. But a big charge also lifts your balance, your utilization, and the amount of cash you need on payment day.

Vehicle purchases deserve extra care. Our post on [leasing versus buying a car](/resources/auto-leasing-vs-buying-which-makes-more-sense) covers mileage, Section 179 limits, and how winery owners can weigh the cash-flow side before putting anything on plastic.

Erin Denney, CPA, our Tax Manager, works with closely-held owner-operators on entity structuring and multi-year tax strategy. Her view of a card strategy is that it sits inside a longer plan. If you expect a profitable year followed by an expansion year, the timing of large purchases, the choice of entity, and the way you pay yourself all affect how much a deduction is worth. Rewards are a nice bonus on top of that plan, not the plan itself.

## A simple way to choose

If you want a short checklist, start here:

- Total your annual card-eligible spending, excluding vendors who add surcharges.
- Find your top two categories and the months they peak.
- Compare a flat-rate card against a category card using your own numbers, including the annual fee.
- Confirm you can pay the full balance each month, even in your tightest month.
- Check that the card exports cleanly into your accounting software, so reconciliation stays quick.

The [SBA's guide to managing your business finances](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/manage-your-business/manage-your-finances) is a useful companion if you are also reviewing credit, budgeting, and cash-flow practices more broadly.

## Common Questions

**Are credit card rewards taxable for a winery?**

Usually not. Cash back and points earned from purchases are generally treated as a rebate, not income. Sign-up bonuses that require no spending, and referral payments, can be taxable. Keep the card statements and any 1099s, and tell us how you record rewards so your books stay consistent.

**What is the best business credit card for a winery?**

The one that matches your spending. If bottling supplies, shipping, and advertising make up most of your spend, a card with bonus categories in those areas may win. If your spending is scattered, a flat 2 percent card with no annual fee is simpler and easier to reconcile.

**Should I pay my grape growers by credit card?**

Only if the grower does not add a surcharge and your rewards exceed any fee. A 3 percent surcharge against a 2 percent reward loses money. Many growers prefer checks or ACH, and those methods keep your credit limit free for harvest costs.
