## How can you protect and pass on what you have built?

Federal estate tax gets most of the headlines, but Washington has its own estate tax, and it starts at a much lower threshold. A family that owes nothing federally can still owe the state. Thresholds change, so we confirm the current numbers with you instead of quoting them from memory.

One point settles a common search: Washington has no inheritance tax. Beneficiaries do not pay a state tax simply for receiving an inheritance. They can still face income tax questions on things like retirement accounts or the sale of inherited property, which is why beneficiary tax planning matters.

Good estate tax planning usually comes down to four things:

- Estimating what your estate could owe at the federal and state level, then modeling ways to lower it.
- Choosing how assets are titled and held, which is the heart of asset protection.
- Tracking basis, the starting value used to calculate taxable gain, so heirs are not overtaxed later.
- Looking past the next generation, since multi-generational wealth planning means thinking about grandchildren, not just children.

Plans also go stale. A marriage, a divorce, a new grandchild, a death in the family, or the sale of a business can all change the math. If your plan was written before one of those, it is worth a second look.

## Wineries, farms, and family businesses that need a succession plan

For a winery or a farm, the estate is rarely cash. It is land, equipment, barrels, and vintages sitting in inventory. When an owner passes away without a plan, heirs can be forced to sell part of the operation just to cover the tax bill. That is a hard way to learn what the estate was worth.

We work with winery owners and farm and ranch families across Eastern Washington on winery estate planning and agricultural estate planning. Business continuity planning and business succession planning are part of the same conversation: who runs it, who owns it, and how the tax works when the keys change hands.

The moments that usually start the conversation:

- A large equipment or land purchase.
- An expansion, like a new tasting room or more acres.
- An owner who is getting older and has not yet named a successor.

Her work helps owner-operators choose how the business is held today, so the transfer tomorrow costs less. Already working with us on your books? ## What do you do when you suddenly become a trustee or administrator?

One phone call, one funeral, or one signed trust, and suddenly you are the trustee. Nobody hands you a manual. You are now a fiduciary, which simply means you are legally responsible for managing someone else's assets with care. Fiduciary tax rules are unfamiliar, basis tracking is confusing, gift tax questions pop up, and it all lands during a difficult season.

We take the accounting and tax weight off your shoulders and explain each step in plain language. If a term needs defining, we define it. Here is what we handle for trust administration in Walla Walla, WA and across Eastern Washington:

- **Fiduciary accounting:** recording what comes in, what goes out, and what you owe beneficiaries.
- **Trust accounting:** clean, ongoing records for trusts that continue after a death.
- **Probate accounting:** the financial reports that go with the court process.
- **Estate settlement:** paying final bills and taxes, then closing things out properly.
- **Trust and estate tax return preparation:** including the fiduciary income tax return and any estate tax filings.

You do not have to figure this out alone. For a deeper look at the tax side, visit our [estate and trust tax administration](/estate-trust-tax-administration) page.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Estate and trust planning services

**Q: Does Washington have an inheritance tax?**
A: No. Washington does not levy an inheritance tax on beneficiaries. It does have its own estate tax, which is paid by the estate itself and kicks in at a much lower threshold than the federal one. Thresholds change, so we confirm current figures with you before building a plan.

**Q: Do I need a CPA if I already have an estate attorney?**
A: Often, yes. Your attorney drafts the will and trust documents. A CPA models how those choices play out in estate tax, income tax, and asset basis, then prepares the returns afterward. We work alongside your attorney so the legal plan and the tax plan point the same direction.

**Q: What does trust administration involve for a new trustee?**
A: A trustee collects and protects assets, pays debts and expenses, keeps accurate records, files tax returns, and distributes to beneficiaries. We handle the accounting and tax side: fiduciary and trust accounting, basis tracking, and trust and estate tax returns, explained in plain language so you know what each step means.

**Q: Can you help families who live out of town?**
A: Yes. Our tax process is secure and paperless, so you can upload documents and e-sign from anywhere. That helps trustees and heirs who live outside Walla Walla or Moses Lake. We schedule calls around your calendar and coordinate with your attorney directly when you want us to.

**Q: When should I update my estate plan?**
A: Revisit it after a marriage, divorce, birth, death, business sale, large land or equipment purchase, or a move. Tax law shifts too, so a plan written years ago may miss today's thresholds. A short review with your CPA and attorney every few years catches gaps before they become costly.
